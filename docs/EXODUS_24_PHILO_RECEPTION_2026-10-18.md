# Philo and the Exodus 24 place clause

## Effect on the working judgment

Philo's received Greek text explicitly quotes the place clause in two passages
of *On Dreams*. This strengthens the literary evidence for its Jewish reception;
it is no longer merely an unexamined reference in a biblical apparatus. It does
not decide whether the clause originated in a Hebrew exemplar or in Greek
translation. Retain POB's Hebrew and main English provisionally, as in the
[preceding comparison](EXODUS_24_VISION_AND_PLACE_COMPARISON_2026-10-18.md).

The [evidence packet](../sources/textual_restoration/comparisons/exodus24_philo.2026-10-18.v1.json)
binds this follow-up to commit `51419088c94861ef18df256d09b5f59232a4be29`.
The earlier report and packet remain unchanged. No canonical, note, reader-code

## The actual quotations and their context

The version-pinned [First1KGreek transcription of On Dreams](https://github.com/OpenGreekAndLatin/First1KGreek/blob/03776b39f4047c5cff06f5296fae4b2bae4b08fb/data/tlg0018/tlg019/tlg0018.tlg019.1st1K-grc1.xml)
was checked against Wendland's printed edition, volume III (1898),
[scan](https://archive.org/details/philonisalexand10wendgoog), printed 218
and 294 / PDF 273 and 361. Both complete pages and their apparatus were inspected.
The surrounding sections read in Greek were 1.60–64 and 2.219–223, not the whole work.

At **1.62** the selected text quotes:

> εἶδον … τὸν τόπον οὗ εἱστήκει ὁ θεὸς τοῦ Ἰσραήλ

Philo distinguishes three senses of place. Here the quotation supports the
divine Word, followed by language about the permitted place of sacrifice.
At **2.222** the same clause supports divine immutability: standing signifies
stability. These different arguments make the place wording integral to the
received exposition, rather than a gemstone-only parallel. Neither quotation
contains **ἐκεῖ** within this clause. Quotation abbreviation remains possible;
this is not a vote that settles the earliest biblical adverb reading. The
**ἐκεῖ** at 2.221 belongs to a different quotation, Exodus 17:6.

The [Greek Confusion of Tongues transcription](https://github.com/OpenGreekAndLatin/First1KGreek/blob/03776b39f4047c5cff06f5296fae4b2bae4b08fb/data/tlg0018/tlg013/tlg0018.tlg013.1st1K-grc1.xml)
was checked against volume II (1897), [scan](https://archive.org/details/philonisalexand00wendgoog),
printed 247–248 / PDF 300–301. Sections 94–98 were read; both complete pages
and apparatus were inspected. Section 96 adapts the place/standing/feet scene;
97–98 interpret divine sight and deny literal bodily members. This is an
interpretive reuse, not another verbatim biblical manuscript. In particular,
the inspected 96 prints **ὃς δῆλός ἐστι**, not **ὃς δὴ λόγος ἐστί**. Do not
silently substitute another edition or English translation at that point.
The apparatus also distinguishes editorial **πλίνθου** from reported manuscript
**λίθου**; this follow-up does not adjudicate the gemstone phrase.

## What this evidence can establish

Three passages by one author are not three independent Exodus manuscripts.
The scanned nineteenth-century editions and their digital transcriptions are
also not independent witnesses. They transmit an ancient author's work, not
newly read first-century physical ink; possible later changes in Philo's own
transmission remain a separate question. No Philo manuscript image or complete
modern Philonic apparatus was consulted here.

The received quotations make a solely late Christian origin of the place
clause harder to maintain without an additional interpolation argument. They
do not recover a Hebrew place clause, prove the translator's motive or certify
Philo's exact biblical exemplar. Philo's interpretation likewise cannot simply
be assigned to the original Exodus translator. This is consequential reception
evidence, not a new discovery or a reason to add Philo to the biblical canon.
