# Jeremiah 39: who authorizes the prophet's release?

Retain POB's longer Hebrew source and English provisionally. A directly checked
Greek edition lacks Jeremiah 39:4–13 in its main text, including Nebuchadnezzar's
explicit release order. That materially changes this episode's presentation,
but does not yet establish which Hebrew form came first. No source, English,
note, approval, reader layout or canon change follows.

## Three distinct evidence layers

References below use Hebrew numbering; Greek chapter 46 corresponds to Hebrew
39. [Swete III, 1905](https://archive.org/details/theoldtestamenti03swetuoft),
printed pp. 334–335 / PDF pp. 358–359, was read completely and viewed, including
the apparatus. This historical edition is not a fresh manuscript-image collation
or a complete modern critical apparatus.

| Passage | Current WLC / POB | Swete's main Greek | Old Latin VL177 as reported by Bogaert |
|---|---|---|---|
| 39:1–2 | Siege chronology present | Present | Absent |
| 39:4–13 | Fall narrative and royal order present | Absent; longer text in Qmg apparatus | Absent |
| 39:14 | Officials act after the royal command | Officials in verse 3 act directly | Release follows verse 3 |
| 39:15 | Oracle introduction present | Present | Four illegible minium lines; French supplied from Greek |

Qmg is Marchalianus's marginal layer, not the main text of every Greek witness.
The long apparatus addition is marked with θʹ and an asterisk; it includes the
royal command in verses 11–12. Do not describe all Greek as omitting this material.
The main Greek verse 14 has ἐξήγαγον αὐτόν, bringing him out; current POB's
Hebrew-based “to bring him home” is not an exact rendering of that Greek clause.

[Bogaert's original 2013 article](https://www.persee.fr/doc/thlou_0080-2654_2013_num_44_2_4122),
pp. 225–229, was read with footnotes, not the whole article. Its reported Latin
sequence is 38:28 → 39:3 → 14 → illegible 15 → 16. Verse 15's supplied rendering
is expressly from Greek, not recovered Latin. Verse 14's `T/Iafret` is uncertain;
Ranke's reading and later proposal are distinguished. Bogaert quotes Ziegler's
`v. 1-2 sub asterisco in OL; om. v. 1-2 LaW`; we leave `OL` unexpanded pending
the actual key. His interpretation links both minuses to a shorter early Greek
and later alignment with expanded Hebrew. This remains an attributed historical
model. His *capitula* argument uses related summaries, not another complete copy;
he expressly concedes they cannot prove the absence of verses 1–2. His cited
late-Hebrew analyses have not been independently read here.

## Consequence and a concrete countertest

Our current-source control checked the source and main English of all 42 records
in Jeremiah 38:24–28, 39:1–18, 40:1–6 and 52:4–16. The longer form places the
release within an explicit royal chain of command and interrupts it with
Zedekiah's fate, destruction and deportation. The shorter Greek connects the
officers' arrival directly to release. This changes narrative emphasis, not
merely English style. Chapter 52 supplies a related fall account, not an exact
duplicate proving the direction of revision.

There is also a specific copying-loss countermodel. Direct written-word
extraction from the WLC OSIS, removing points and accents, finds both 39:3 and
39:13 end with `מלך בבל`. A hypothetical Hebrew copyist's jump between those
endings would produce the 3 → 14 sequence. This is our conditional mechanism,
not a demonstrated ancient copying event: the formula is short, the exemplar's
layout is unknown, and no probability is assigned. It does not by itself
explain VL177's additional absence of verses 1–2. Nor does Swete's Qmg verse 13
end with that same full Hebrew officials formula; no identical Greek eye-skip
is established by these pages.

The Greek continuation at 47:1–5 / Hebrew 40:1–5 still contains Nebuzaradan's
release, chains and speech. Hebrew has Ramah; Swete's main Greek prints
ἐκ Δαμὰν, with Ραμα A, Δραμα Q and Ρεμαθ Qmg in the apparatus.
Therefore the shorter chapter 39 does
not remove him from the whole narrative or eliminate every release tension.
Coherence can support an edition's intelligibility without dating its origin.
