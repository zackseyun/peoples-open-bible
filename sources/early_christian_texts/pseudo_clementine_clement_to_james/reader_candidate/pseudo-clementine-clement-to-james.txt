SECTION 1

Clement to James, the lord and bishop of bishops, who governs the holy Jerusalem church of the Hebrews and the churches everywhere that have been well established by God’s providence, together with the elders and deacons and all the other brothers: may peace always be.

Let it be known to you, my lord, that Simon—who, because of his true faith and the most secure basis of his teaching, was designated to be the foundation of the church and, for this very reason, was renamed Peter by Jesus himself with his unlying mouth; the firstfruits of our Lord; the first of the apostles; the one to whom the Father first revealed the Son; the one whom the Messiah rightly pronounced blessed; the called and chosen one, table companion and fellow traveler; the good and proven disciple; the one commanded to illuminate the West, the darker part of the world, as more capable than everyone else, and who proved able to accomplish it; and indeed, how long shall I prolong the account, unwilling to disclose the painful thing that necessity compels me, even if late, in any event to tell?—this same man, because of his boundless affection for humanity, having clearly and publicly proclaimed to the whole world, under the present evil one, the future good king, and having come as far as here, to Rome, saving people through teaching willed by God, himself left this present life through violence.

SECTION 2

Now, in the very days when he was about to die, after the brothers had assembled, he suddenly took hold of my hand, stood up before the church, and said, “Listen to me, brothers and fellow servants. Since—as I was taught by the Lord and Teacher Jesus Christ, who sent me—the days of my death have drawn near, I ordain this Clement as bishop for you. To him I entrust my chair of teaching. He accompanied me from the beginning to the end and thus listened to all my discourses; to speak in summary, he shared in all my trials and was found persevering in faith. More than anyone else, I have found by experience that he is God-revering, a lover of humanity, pure, widely learned, self-controlled, good, just, patient, and knows how to bear bravely the acts of ingratitude committed by some of those receiving instruction. Therefore I impart to him the authority to bind and release, so that, concerning whatever he ordains on earth, it will stand decreed in the heavens. For he will bind what must be bound and release what must be released, since he knows the church’s rule. Listen to him, therefore, recognizing that whoever grieves the one who presides over truth sins against the Messiah and provokes the Father of all; for this reason, that person will not live. Therefore the one who presides must himself occupy the place of a physician, not possess the rage of an irrational beast.”

SECTION 3

As he was saying these things, I fell before him and pleaded with him, declining the honor and authority of the chair. But he answered, “Do not ask this of me, for I have resolved that this should happen—and all the more for this very reason, if you decline it—because such a chair does not need a rash man eager to occupy the chair, but one who is circumspect in character and widely learned in discourse. Produce someone better, one who accompanied me more than you, listened to my words, and thoroughly learned the governance of the church, and I will not force you, against your will, to do what is right. But you will not now be able to produce anyone better than yourself, for you are the superior firstfruit of the nations being saved through me. Nevertheless, consider this other point as well: if, fearing the danger of sin, you do not undertake the governance of the church, know well that you sin more, since, though able to help those who revere God, as people sailing and in danger, you refuse, considering only your own interest and not what benefits everyone in common. I understand correctly that you must, in any event, undertake the danger, since I do not cease asking this of you for the help of everyone. Therefore, the sooner you agree with me, the more you will relieve me of discouragement.”

The translation connects it with σωζομένων, yielding ‘the nations being saved through me.’ It can instead modify the main assertion: ‘through me you are the superior firstfruit of the nations being saved.’ The supplied wording requires no textual correction.

SECTION 4

“And I myself also know, Clement, that I am bestowing on you griefs, discouragements, dangers, and reproaches from uneducated crowds. You will be able to bear these bravely by keeping in view the great reward from God that will be paid to you for your endurance. But also, as is right, consider together with me: when does the Messiah need your alliance—now, when the evil one has taken up war against his bride, or in the coming time, when, having conquered, he reigns and no longer needs any help? Is it not clear even to someone with little understanding that it is now? Therefore, with full resolve, hurry in this present time of need to ally yourself with a good king, whose nature it is to pay great rewards after victory. Gladly, then, exercise oversight—all the more suitably in this respect, since you have learned from me how to govern the church—for the salvation of the brothers who flee to us for refuge.”

SECTION 5

“Nevertheless, before everyone and for everyone’s sake, I also wish to remind you briefly about the matters of governance. You must live blamelessly and, with the greatest diligence, shake off all the occupations of life, becoming neither a guarantor nor an advocate and becoming entangled in no other worldly affair. For the Messiah does not want you appointed as a judge and adjudicator over money or business among the present affairs of worldly life and thereby, absorbed in people’s present concerns, left without leisure to separate, by the word of truth, the better people from the worse. Rather, let those who are learning provide these things for one another, and let them not distract you from the words capable of saving. For just as it is impious for you to undertake worldly concerns while neglecting to do what you were commanded, so it is a sin for each layperson if they do not stand by one another also in worldly needs. Moreover, if all of them do not understand about which matters they ought to keep you free from concern, let them learn from the deacons, so that you may care only for the church, both governing it well and providing the words of truth.”

Translator's notes for section 5 (not part of the ancient text):

1. Greek: οὐ γὰρ κριτὴν καὶ δικαστὴν χρημάτων ἢ ἀσχολημάτων καθεστάναι σε θέλει τῶν νῦν βιωτικῶν πραγμάτων ὁ χριστός, ἵνα συνεχόμενος εἰς τὰς νῦν τῶν ἀνθρώπων φροντίδας μὴ εὐσχολῇς χωρίζειν λόγῳ ἀληθείας ἀνθρώπων τοὺς κρείττονας ἀπὸ τῶν χειρόνων
The scope of the unusual οὐ ... θέλει ... ἵνα ... μὴ construction is interpretively difficult. The translation takes the ἵνα clause as the unwanted outcome included within the Messiah’s negated desire: appointment to worldly adjudication would absorb Clement and leave him without leisure for his teaching task. A more formally sequential rendering with ‘so that’ could obscure this scope or suggest that the lack of leisure is the purpose of refusing the appointment. No textual correction is proposed.

2. Greek: καὶ σὲ δὲ περὶ ὧν χρὴ ἀμέριμνον εἶναι οἱ πάντες ποιεῖν ἐὰν μὴ νοῶσιν, παρὰ τῶν διακόνων μανθανέτωσαν

SECTION 6

For if you become occupied with the cares of ordinary life, you will lay an ambush for both yourself and your hearers. For because your occupations leave you unable to furnish what is beneficial as provisions, you will be punished for not teaching what is beneficial, while those who did not learn will perish, ignorance being the cause. Therefore, give them your undistracted time and preside over them, so that at the right time you may provide words capable of saving them. And so let them heed you, knowing that whatever the ambassador of the truth binds on earth has also been bound in heaven, and whatever he looses has been loosed. You, however, will bind what must be bound and loose what must be loosed. And as for the matters concerning you, the presiding officer, these are they, along with things like them.

SECTION 7

But let the duties concerning the elders be these: above all, let them quickly join the young in marriage, forestalling the snares of youthful desire. But let them not neglect marriage even for those already old, for in some people desire remains vigorous even after they have grown old. Therefore, so that sexual immorality may not gain ground against you and cast the same plague into your midst, take precautions beforehand and investigate whether the fire of adultery may be springing up secretly among you. For adultery is exceedingly terrible—so much so that it holds second place in punishment, since first place is awarded to those who are in error, even if they are chaste. Therefore, as elders of the church, train the Messiah’s bride in chastity (and by “bride” I mean the corporate body of the church). For if she is found chaste by the bridegroom king, she will obtain the greatest honor, and you, as those invited to the wedding, will enjoy great rejoicing. But if she is discovered to have sinned, she will be cast out, and you will pay the penalty—lest perhaps the sin have come about through your neglect.

SECTION 8

Therefore, above all, concern yourselves with chastity, for sexual immorality has been decreed exceedingly grave in God’s sight. There are many kinds of sexual immorality, as Clement himself will also explain to you. But the first is adultery: a man’s not having relations only with his own wife, and a woman’s not having relations only with her own husband. If someone is chaste, he can also come to love humanity, for which reason he will obtain eternal mercy. Just as adultery is a great evil, so love for humanity is the greatest good. Therefore love all your brothers with reverent and merciful eyes, doing for orphans what parents do and for widows what husbands do, providing sustenance with complete gladness, marriages for those in their prime, and, for those among them without a trade, devising through occupations the means of necessary sustenance: work for a craftsperson, mercy for someone unable to work.

SECTION 9

I know that you will do these things if you establish love in your mind. There is one sufficient occasion for its entry: the common sharing of salt. Therefore make every effort to dine with one another more frequently, as you are able, so that you do not barter it away; for it is the cause of doing good, and doing good is the cause of salvation. All of you, then, make your means of life common to everyone who is a brother according to God, knowing that by giving temporary things you will receive eternal things. But, so that I do not speak item by item, love for humanity will teach you to do every good thing, just as hatred of humanity points out the practice of evil to those who do not wish to be saved.

SECTION 10

Brothers who have disputes must not have their cases judged before the authorities, but must in every way be brought to agreement by the elders of the church, readily obeying them. Moreover, flee greed as something capable, under the pretext of temporary gain, of depriving you of eternal good things. Do you carefully observe balances, measures, and weights—the lawful standards of the localities? Act honorably with regard to deposits entrusted to you. Moreover, you will persevere in doing these and similar things to the end if you keep in your hearts an inseparable remembrance of the judgment that comes from God. The supplied print joins the article and noun without a space. This is an orthographic word-division feature and does not by itself create substantive uncertainty.

The supplied punctuation presents the sentence as a question, although φυλάσσετε can formally be read as either indicative or imperative. The translation retains the question. Syntactically, τὰ τῶντόπων δίκαια may appositionally identify the preceding balances, measures, and weights as the lawful local standards, as translated, or it may be a separate fourth object referring more generally to the lawful requirements of the localities.

SECTION 12

SECTION 14

For the whole enterprise of the church resembles a large ship carrying through a fierce storm men who are from many places and who wish to inhabit one particular city belonging to a good kingdom. Let God, then, be the master of this ship for you, and let the helmsman be likened to the Messiah, the bow officer to the bishop, the sailors to the elders, the officers of the ship’s sides to the deacons, the ship’s recruiters to those who catechize, the multitude of the brothers to the passengers, the world to the deep sea, and the contrary winds to the temptations. SECTION 15

2. Greek: ὡς πρωρεὺς ’ἐγρηγορὼς
The raised mark before ἐγρηγορὼς is not required by the apparent syntax or ordinary elision. Its typographic or editorial function is uncertain, but it does not prevent a defensible translation of the surrounding words.

3. Greek: τοῦ κυβερνήτου μόνου τοὺς λόγους ἀντιβαλλέτω
The exact action of ἀντιβαλλέτω is lexically open. The main text chooses “repeat,” taking the helmsman’s words as what the bow officer gives back or reproduces. “Answer with” or “exchange” has support from conversational usage, while “relay” would fit the nautical setting but adds an unexpressed onward recipient. The selected rendering is defensible without altering the Greek.

4. Greek: ὁ χριστὸς ὡς σωτὴρ κυβερνήτης φιλείσθω
The single ὡς introduces the adjacent nouns σωτήρ and κυβερνήτης without an expressed conjunction. The translation coordinates them in idiomatic English as “Savior and helmsman.” They may alternatively be heard as a combined designation, “Savior-helmsman,” or as “the saving helmsman.”

5. Greek: ἐνούμενοι
The supplied form has smooth breathing. The context contrasts scattering with being united and supports identification with the unity verb conventionally written ἑνόω. This is an intelligible orthographic feature, not grounds for treating the verified print as unreadable.

6. Greek: ἀπερῶντες
The form is uncommon, but its purgative sense is made intelligible by the author’s immediate explanation involving disease-producing bile and the related form ἀπεράσαντες later in the same sentence. The exact lexical formation remains uncertain, while the contextual action is defensibly rendered as vomiting.

7. Translation choice — Greek: εὐσταθείτωσαν οὖν οἱ ἐπιβάται ἑδραῖοι ἐπὶ τῶν ἰδίων καθεζόμενοι τόπων
Rendered as: The passengers, then, should remain stable, seated firmly in their own places
Preserves both the demand for stability and the passengers’ firm occupation of their respective places.

8. Translation choice — Greek: σεισμὸν ἢ ἑτεροκλινίαν
Rendered as: rocking or a list to one side
Distinguishes general shaking from an unequal inclination of the ship toward one side.

9. Translation choice — Greek: οἱ ναυστολόγοι τοὺς μισθοὺς ὑπομιμνησκέτωσαν
Rendered as: The ship’s recruiting officers should remind them of the wages
Ναυστολόγοι denotes those who enroll or recruit a ship’s company; the recipient of the reminder is implicit, while μισθοί retains the image of promised wages or rewards.

10. Translation choice — Greek: καταρτιζέτωσαν ἐπιμελῶς τὰ χρῄζοντα ἑκάστῳ
Rendered as: should carefully prepare what each person needs
Treats τὰ χρῄζοντα as the necessary provisions or arrangements appropriate to each person.

11. Translation choice — Greek: ὡς πρωρεὺς ’ἐγρηγορὼς
Rendered as: as a watchful bow officer
Πρωρεύς is the officer stationed toward the bow, while ἐγρηγορώς emphasizes continued watchfulness.

12. Translation choice — Greek: τοῦ κυβερνήτου μόνου τοὺς λόγους ἀντιβαλλέτω
Rendered as: should repeat only the helmsman’s words
Takes the helmsman’s words as the direct object and understands ἀντιβάλλω as giving them back or reproducing them, fitting the bishop’s subordinate role in the ship analogy.

13. Translation choice — Greek: ὁ χριστὸς ὡς σωτὴρ κυβερνήτης
Rendered as: The Messiah should be loved as Savior and helmsman
Renders the independent title χριστός as “Messiah” and preserves both adjacent designations, σωτήρ and κυβερνήτης.

14. Translation choice — Greek: ναυτιῶντες, ἰλιγγιῶντες, ἀπερῶντες
Rendered as: seasick, dizzy, and vomiting
The author’s ensuing explanation in terms of expelling disease-producing bile strongly supports a purgative or vomiting sense for the uncommon final form.

15. Translation choice — Greek: προσιέμενοι τὴν ἐκ τῆς ἐπιμελείας σωτήριον ὑγείαν
Rendered as: receive the saving health that comes from attentive care
Preserves the medical image of accepting restorative health produced by care or treatment.

SECTION 16

Nevertheless, all of you should recognize that the bishop toils more than all of you, because each of you suffers his own affliction, whereas he suffers both his own and that of each person. Therefore, Clement, preside as a helper to each person, as far as you are able, burdened with the cares of everyone. Accordingly, in your undertaking to administer, I know that I have received a favor, not given one. But take courage and bear it nobly, knowing that when you have reached the harbor of rest, God will give you in return the greatest of good things—an inalienable reward—because you undertook the greater toil for the salvation of all. Thus, if many of the brothers hate you because of righteousness carried to the utmost, their hatred will do you no harm, but the friendship of the righteous God will benefit you greatly. Therefore, strive to shake off praise arising from injustice, but through just administration to hunt for the beneficial praise that comes from the Messiah.

Translator's notes for section 16 (not part of the ancient text):

1. Greek: τὸ σὲ οἰκονομεῖν ἀναδέξασθαι χάριν οἶδα εἰληφώς, οὐ δεδωκώς
The main rendering treats σέ as the accusative subject of the entire infinitival expression, “your undertaking to administer,” while the nominative participles εἰληφώς and δεδωκώς refer to the first-person speaker, who knows himself to have received rather than given a favor. An alternative analysis makes τὸ σὲ οἰκονομεῖν the object of ἀναδέξασθαι, with the speaker undertaking the arrangement that Clement administer. A less contextually suitable reading takes σέ as the direct object of οἰκονομεῖν, so that the speaker undertakes to administer Clement. The explicit accusative can defensibly be the embedded subject, and the immediate commission to Clement supports that choice; no emendation is required.

2. Greek: ’σωτηρίας
The supplied print places a raised mark before σωτηρίας although no ordinary elision is apparent. The mark does not affect the intelligible phrase ὑπὲρ τῆς τῶν πάντων σωτηρίας and is treated as a nonlexical editorial or typographic feature.

3. Greek: πλὴν γινώσκετε πάντες πάντων ὑμῶν πλεῖον κάμνοντα τὸν ἐπίσκοπον
γινώσκετε can be indicative (“all of you know”) or imperative (“all of you should recognize”). The main text adopts the exhortation because it stands amid directives about supporting the bishop; an appeal to knowledge the hearers already possess remains a defensible alternative. The bishop is expressed by an accusative noun with the agreeing participle κάμνοντα, not by an infinitive.

4. Translation choice — Greek: πάντων ὑμῶν πλεῖον κάμνοντα τὸν ἐπίσκοπον
Rendered as: the bishop toils more than all of you
γινώσκετε takes the accusative τὸν ἐπίσκοπον with its supplementary or predicative participle κάμνοντα. The phrase compares the bishop’s wearisome labor with that of the hearers; this is not an accusative–infinitive construction.

5. Translation choice — Greek: ἕκαστος ὑμῶν τὴν αὐτοῦ θλίψιν πάσχει, αὐτὸς δὲ τὴν αὐτοῦ καὶ τὴν ἑκάστου
Rendered as: each of you suffers his own affliction, whereas he suffers both his own and that of each person
Preserves the contrast between each member’s individual distress and the bishop’s additional burden for every member.

6. Translation choice — Greek: προκαθέσθητι βοηθός
Rendered as: preside as a helper
Combines Clement’s leading position with the explicitly stated role of helping those under his care.

7. Translation choice — Greek: τὰς πάντων φορτισθεὶς φροντίδας
Rendered as: burdened with the cares of everyone
The passive participle portrays Clement as loaded with the collective concerns of the community.

8. Translation choice — Greek: τὸ σὲ οἰκονομεῖν ἀναδέξασθαι
Rendered as: in your undertaking to administer
Treats σέ as the accusative subject of the whole infinitival expression: Clement undertakes the administration just assigned to him. This accords with the immediate commission and does not force σέ to be the object of οἰκονομεῖν.

9. Translation choice — Greek: ἀναφαίρετον μισθόν
Rendered as: an inalienable reward
The adjective describes a reward that cannot be removed or taken away.

10. Translation choice — Greek: ἐπ’ ἄκρον δικαιοσύνην
Rendered as: righteousness carried to the utmost
Preserves the expression’s image of righteousness reaching its furthest extent.

11. Translation choice — Greek: τὸν ἐκ χριστοῦ ἐπωφελῆ θηρεύειν ἔπαινον
Rendered as: to hunt for the beneficial praise that comes from the Messiah
Retains the hunting image of θηρεύω, the advantageous character of the praise, and Χριστός as an independent title.

SECTION 17

After saying these things and more than these, he again looked toward the multitude and said, “And you also, my beloved brothers and fellow servants, yield in every respect to the one presiding over truth, knowing this: whoever grieves this man has not received the Messiah, with whose chair he has been entrusted; and as one who has not received the Messiah, he will be reckoned as having rejected the Father. Therefore, he will be cast out of the good kingdom. For this reason, strive to come to every gathering, so that you may not, as deserters, incur a charge of sin because of the commander’s discouragement. Therefore, above all else, all of you should give thought to his interests, knowing that, because of each of you, the evil one wages war against him alone with greater hostility. So strive to continue in affection toward him and goodwill toward one another, and to obey him, so that he too may be relieved and you may be able to be saved.”

SECTION 18

But you should also understand some things on your own, because he is unable to speak openly on account of the plots. For example, if he is hostile to someone, do not wait for him to say, “Do not have dealings with this person.” Instead, prudently follow his intention, becoming enemies of those to whom he is hostile and not associating with those with whom he does not associate, so that each person, laying claim to having everyone as friends, may be saved by reconciling him and heeding his discourse. But if anyone remains a friend of those to whom he is hostile and speaks with those with whom he does not associate, that person too is one of those who want to destroy the church. For although he is with you in body, since he is not with you in purpose, he is against you—much worse than the enemies who are visibly outside, scattering those inside while maintaining seeming friendship.

Translator's notes for section 18 (not part of the ancient text):

1. Greek: ἵνα ἕκαστος, ἐπιδικαζόμενος τοῦ πάντας φίλους ἔχειν, αὐτὸν διαλλάττων σώζηται
The main rendering understands ἕκαστος as each person who has become isolated through hostility toward the leader, and αὐτόν as the leader: wishing to retain everyone as friends, that person reconciles the leader and is saved. This follows the paragraph’s repeated singular reference to the leader and explains the communal withdrawal as pressure toward reconciliation. Grammatically, active διαλλάττων makes αὐτόν the affected object but leaves the other party to the reconciliation unexpressed. “Winning him back” or “effecting reconciliation with him” are viable contextual formulations, while identifying αὐτόν as a different alienated person is less contextually natural. The selected construction is defensible without supplying a named referent in the text.

2. Greek: τῆς αὐτοῦ ἐπακούων ὁμιλίας
The main text takes αὐτοῦ as the presiding leader, continuing the repeated references to his intention, hostility, and associations. It could grammatically resume another referent assigned to the preceding αὐτόν, but the leader remains the most continuous and contextually relevant antecedent. The English retains the pronoun rather than replacing it with an explicit name or office.

3. Translation choice — Greek: ἔνια δὲ καὶ ἀφ᾽ ἑαυτῶν νοεῖν ὀφείλετε
Rendered as: you should also understand some things on your own
Preserves the demand that the hearers infer matters for themselves rather than waiting for an explicit public statement.

4. Translation choice — Greek: ἐν φανερῷ λέγειν
Rendered as: speak openly
The phrase refers to public or unconcealed speech, contrasted with intentions that must be inferred because of plots.

5. Translation choice — Greek: προσαμιλλᾶσθαι
Rendered as: have dealings with
The verb can denote engaging, associating, or contending with someone; the surrounding language of friendship and conversation supports the broad relational rendering.

6. Translation choice — Greek: ἀλλὰ φρονίμως ἕπεσθε αὐτοῦ τῷ βουλήματι
Rendered as: Instead, prudently follow his intention
The hearers are told to discern and conform to the leader’s will even when he cannot state it openly.

7. Translation choice — Greek: ἐπιδικαζόμενος τοῦ πάντας φίλους ἔχειν
Rendered as: laying claim to having everyone as friends
The middle verb presents the person as asserting or pursuing a claim to the state expressed by the genitive infinitival phrase.

8. Translation choice — Greek: αὐτὸν διαλλάττων
Rendered as: reconciling him
Preserves αὐτόν as the explicit direct object of the active participle rather than silently changing the construction into self-reconciliation.

9. Translation choice — Greek: τῆς αὐτοῦ ἐπακούων ὁμιλίας
Rendered as: heeding his discourse
The recurring singular pronouns most naturally continue to refer to the presiding leader whose intention and associations govern the paragraph.

10. Translation choice — Greek: τῇ γνώμῃ μὴ ὢν μεθ᾽ ὑμῶν
Rendered as: since he is not with you in purpose
Contrasts bodily presence with inward judgment, intention, or allegiance.

SECTION 19

After saying these things, there in the midst before everyone, he laid his hands on me and entreated me to sit in his own chair. When I had sat down, he immediately said this to me: “Before all my brothers who are present with me, I ask you that, whenever I depart from the life here (as is owed to me), you write a concise account and send it to James, the Lord’s brother, reaching even as far back as your thoughts from childhood and telling how, from the beginning until now, you have traveled with me, heeding the words and deeds proclaimed by me city by city. Then, toward the end, do not hesitate also to disclose the cause of my death, as I said beforehand. For this will not grieve him greatly, since he knows that what I inevitably had to suffer I discharged devoutly. Rather, he will obtain the greatest consolation when he learns that after me no unlearned man—ignorant of life-giving words and not knowing the church’s rule—was entrusted with the teacher’s chair. For discourse that leads astray destroys the souls of the crowds who hear it.”

Translator's notes for section 19 (not part of the ancient text):

1. Greek: καθεσθῆναί με. ἐδυσώπησεν
The printed period separates the infinitive from the finite verb that most naturally governs it. The translation construes them together as “entreated me to sit.” This treats the punctuation as an editorial feature of the supplied edition, not as proof that the Greek words are corrupt.

2. Greek: συνπαρόντων
The supplied edition prints the unassimilated spelling συνπαρόντων. It is intelligible as the form conventionally normalized συμπαρόντων and does not impede translation.

3. Greek: Ἀξιῶ σε ἐπὶ πάντων μου τῶν συνπαρόντων ἀδελφῶν, ὅπως ὁπόταν τοῦ ἐνταῦθα (ὡς ἐμοὶ ὀφείλεται) ἀπαλλάξω βίου, σὲ Ἰακώβῳ τῷ ἀδελφῷ τοῦ κυρίου διαπέμψαι ἐν ἐπιτομῇ ἀναγραψάμενον
The main construction takes the second σέ as a resumed accusative subject after the intervening temporal clause, with ἀναγραψάμενον agreeing with Clement and διαπέμψαι denoting his dispatch of the resulting account to James. The following section’s statement that Clement made and sent the epitome supports this role assignment. Alternatively, σέ could formally be taken as the object of διαπέμψαι, “send you,” but that would require the speaker to remain the unexpressed subject and fits the ensuing account less well. The object sent is unexpressed in Greek and is supplied in English from the writing of the epitome.

4. Greek: ἔπειτα πρὸς τῷ τέλει καὶ τὴν τοῦ θανάτου μου πρόφασιν ὡς προεῖπον δηλῶσαι μὴ ὀκνήσῃς
The main text takes the final μὴ ὀκνήσῃς directly with δηλῶσαι: after the earlier request to write and send the account, Clement is specifically told not to hesitate to disclose the cause of the death near its end. It is also possible to give “do not hesitate” wider scope over both the dispatch and the final disclosure. The selected narrower attachment follows the immediate position of δηλῶσαι and yields a defensible sequence of instructions.

5. Greek: οὐκ ἀμαθὴς ἀνὴρ καὶ ζωοποιοὺς λόγους ἀγνοῶν καὶ ἐκκλησίας κανόνα. οὐκ εἰδὼς τὴν τοῦ διδάσκοντος ἐπιστεύθη καθέδραν
The first οὐκ can be read with broad propositional scope: it was not a man characterized as unlearned and ignorant who was entrusted with the chair. This is the main rendering and coheres with James’s resulting consolation. A strictly local reading produces “a not-unlearned man” who nevertheless is ignorant of life-giving words and the church’s rule, an awkward contrast. Another possible analysis distributes the first negation over the coordinated descriptions, yielding the positive sense that the successor was neither unlearned nor ignorant. The explicit second negation in οὐκ εἰδώς is retained as “not knowing the church’s rule.” The ambiguity concerns scope, not an unreadable source.

6. Greek: κανόνα. οὐκ εἰδὼς
The printed period interrupts the sequence of descriptions before οὐκ εἰδώς. The translation treats the participle as continuing the characterization of the man entrusted with the chair. The punctuation is preserved as a disclosed editorial feature and does not by itself establish corruption or require a conjectural reading.

7. Greek: καὶ ζωοποιοὺς λόγους ἀγνοῶν καὶ ἐκκλησίας κανόνα. οὐκ εἰδὼς
The main text attaches ζωοποιοὺς λόγους to ἀγνοῶν and ἐκκλησίας κανόνα to οὐκ εἰδώς, preserving the two distinct formulations for ignorance. An alternative groups both objects with ἀγνοῶν and takes οὐκ εἰδώς absolutely (“without knowledge”). The main attachment is more direct despite the printed period; that punctuation and the separate scope of the first negation remain disclosed rather than silently corrected.

8. Translation choice — Greek: ἐν μέσῳ ἐπὶ πάντων
Rendered as: there in the midst before everyone
Preserves both the public location in the assembly’s midst and the presence of all as witnesses.

9. Translation choice — Greek: καθεσθῆναί με. ἐδυσώπησεν
Rendered as: entreated me to sit
Construes the infinitive with the following finite verb despite the printed period, yielding the natural sense of earnestly urging Clement to take the chair.

10. Translation choice — Greek: Ἀξιῶ σε
Rendered as: I ask you
Introduces a formal request made before the assembled brothers.

11. Translation choice — Greek: σὲ Ἰακώβῳ τῷ ἀδελφῷ τοῦ κυρίου διαπέμψαι ἐν ἐπιτομῇ ἀναγραψάμενον
Rendered as: you write a concise account and send it to James, the Lord’s brother
Treats the resumed σέ as the accusative subject of διαπέμψαι and ἀναγραψάμενον. English supplies “account” and “it” from the act of writing in epitome because διαπέμπω otherwise lacks an expressed object.

12. Translation choice — Greek: μέχρι καὶ τῶν ἐκ παίδων σου λογισμῶν
Rendered as: reaching even as far back as your thoughts from childhood
Presents the requested record as extending to Clement’s earliest stated thoughts or reflections.

13. Translation choice — Greek: τῶν κατὰ πόλιν ὑπ᾽ ἐμοῦ κηρυχθέντων λόγων τε καὶ πράξεων
Rendered as: the words and deeds proclaimed by me city by city
Retains the unusual coordination in which κηρυχθέντων grammatically characterizes both the words and the deeds.

14. Translation choice — Greek: τὴν τοῦ θανάτου μου πρόφασιν
Rendered as: the cause of my death
Πρόφασις can denote cause, occasion, or pretext; an account of Peter’s death supports “cause” here.

15. Translation choice — Greek: ὃ πάντως ἔδει με παθεῖν εὐσεβῶς ἀπεδόμην
Rendered as: what I inevitably had to suffer I discharged devoutly
Preserves the necessary suffering as an obligation that the speaker has paid or fulfilled in a devout manner.

16. Translation choice — Greek: οὐκ ἀμαθὴς ἀνὴρ καὶ ζωοποιοὺς λόγους ἀγνοῶν καὶ ἐκκλησίας κανόνα. οὐκ εἰδὼς τὴν τοῦ διδάσκοντος ἐπιστεύθη καθέδραν
Rendered as: no unlearned man—ignorant of life-giving words and not knowing the church’s rule—was entrusted with the teacher’s chair
The main text pairs life-giving words with ἀγνοῶν and the church’s rule with οὐκ εἰδώς. The alternative shared-object/absolute-participle attachment and the printed punctuation are disclosed separately; both source negations remain represented.

17. Translation choice — Greek: πλάνος γὰρ ὁμιλία
Rendered as: For discourse that leads astray
Renders πλάνος by its active force of causing hearers to wander or be deceived.

SECTION 20

Therefore I, my lord James, after he had said these things and I had made my promise, did not hesitate, as I had been commanded, to present in outline the greater part of the discourses from city to city, which had already been written to you beforehand and sent by him in books, as a kind of reminder, and in this form to send them to you, giving the work the title “Clement’s Epitome of Peter’s Preachings among the People.” But I will begin to set it out as I was commanded.

Translator's notes for section 20 (not part of the ancient text):

1. Greek: ἐπιγράψαντα
The accusative participle is syntactically marked with the first-person subject of οὐκ ὤκνησα. It may agree with an understood accusative subject of the dependent infinitives. The nominative ἐπιγράψας would be a conjectural normalization, not an established correction to the supplied reading.

2. Greek: Κλήμεντος τῶν Πέτρου ἐπιδημίων κηρυγμάτων ἐπιτομή
The title renders ἐπιδημίων with the broad adjective sense “among the people,” attested in LSJ under ἐπιδήμιος (including Iliad 24.262 and the resident-merchants usage in Herodotus 2.39). Local or public preaching is a possible contextual emphasis. An association with Peter’s visits is suggested by the preceding city-by-city description, but a specific sojourning sense is not asserted from cognate words alone. These alternatives remain interpretive, not proposed changes to the Greek.

3. Translation choice — Greek: ὑποσχόμενος
Rendered as: I had made my promise
The participle records Clement’s promise before describing his prompt fulfillment of it.

4. Translation choice — Greek: τὸ πολὺ
Rendered as: the greater part
The expression denotes the bulk or most of the material rather than necessarily all of it.

5. Translation choice — Greek: ἐπὶ κεφαλαίων ποιῆσαι
Rendered as: to present in outline
The phrase concerns reducing or presenting the material by principal points, not merely placing otherwise unabridged material beneath headings.

6. Translation choice — Greek: σημείου χάριν
Rendered as: as a kind of reminder
Literally involving a sign or marker, the phrase describes the summary as an aid to recognition or recollection.

7. Translation choice — Greek: ἐπιδημίων κηρυγμάτων
Rendered as: Clement’s Epitome of Peter’s Preachings among the People
The adjective ἐπιδήμιος itself has the LSJ sense “among the people,” with community, local and resident associations. This broader wording avoids deriving a specific sojourning meaning merely from cognate nouns or verbs. The preceding city-by-city account supplies context, not a lexical equation with travel.

8. Translation choice — Greek: ἐκτιθέναι
Rendered as: set it out
The verb marks the transition from the prefatory letter to presenting the promised material.

End of Pseudo-Clementine Letter of Clement to James.
