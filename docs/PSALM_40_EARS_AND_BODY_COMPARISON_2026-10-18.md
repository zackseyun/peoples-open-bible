# Psalm 40 ears and body comparison

## Result

Retain the Hebrew **אזנים כרית לי**, provisionally represented by POB's
“ears you have opened for me.” The Greek **σῶμα** is a real, consequential
reading, not evidence by itself for a different Hebrew noun. Its priority
within the Greek Psalter remains unresolved in this comparison. Hebrews
10:5 should retain its independently attested Greek “body”; neither passage
should be harmonized to the other.

This is a new connected comparison, not a new discovery of the variant.
No canonical source, main English, footnote or implementation changes here.
The [evidence packet](../sources/textual_restoration/comparisons/psalm40_ears_body.2026-10-18.v1.json)
pins inputs and consultation scopes. The earlier
[Psalm 22 control](PSALM_22_SOURCE_COMPARISON_PASS_1.md) checked a Greek verb,
not this noun's textual history.

The existing Psalms map specifies a one-verse offset for this chapter.
Actual `wlc.load_verse` results match the three canonical source fields.
Their connected Hebrew moves from unwanted offerings and the giving of ears,
through the speaker's coming and the written scroll, to delight in God's
will and instruction within the speaker. The repeated חפץ root in the first
and third units links divine desire and the speaker's delight. This supports
an obedient-response interpretation without replacing the concrete ear image.

Isaiah 50:5 has **פתח**, “opened,” and a singular ear. Exodus 21:6 has
**רצע**, a singular ear and an awl; Deuteronomy 15:17 also explicitly names
the awl and ear. Psalm 40 instead has **כרה** and dual ears, without an awl
or door. Those parallels help interpretation but do not establish that the
Psalm describes the slave-piercing ceremony or that “dug” simply means “opened.”
POB's idiomatic English remains provisional; this comparison does not certify
it as the optimal expression of the image.

The pinned QDR biblical index returns no hits at Hebrew 40:7–9. Its nearby
40:1–2 entries concern 11Q8, not this clause. This is a partial-index screen,
not an exhaustive absence claim or preserved-letter inspection of 11Q8.
No consulted direct Hebrew evidence supplies a noun meaning “body.”

## The Greek is not one uniform reading

Root visually inspected Swete II, printed 264 / PDF 282, its complete target
apparatus, sigla key at PDF 18, and preface at PDF 10. The selected text has
**σῶμα δὲ κατηρτίσω μοι**, singular **ὁλοκαύτωμα**, and **ᾔτησας**.
The apparatus reports plural burnt offerings in A/R/T and **ἐζήτησας**
in Sinaiticus/A/R/T. At 39:9 its selected “heart” contrasts with the reported
“belly” in Sinaiticus/A/T. These are edition reports, not newly read manuscript
hands; R is the Veronese Greek-Latin Psalter and T the Zurich Psalter.

The reacquired, hash-matching OpenScriptorium
[Rahlfs-derived control](https://github.com/OpenScriptorium/lxx-morph/blob/c91f6b1e8fb3ba37df701e6ae31f675ace71a2b2/db/seeds/lxx_morph/psalms-lxx.json)
instead prints **ὠτία**, ears, with **κατηρτίσω**, singular burnt offering,
and **ᾔτησας**; 39:9 has **κοιλίας**, belly. Only surface fields were used,
not its machine morphology or reasoning. This control is an edition, not
another ancient witness or the complete Göttingen apparatus.

The actual local SBLGNT source at Hebrews 10:5–10 has body, plural burnt
offerings, **εὐδόκησας**, and a quotation ending without the Psalm's
**ἐβουλήθην** and internal-law clause. The commentary at 10:9 directly joins
coming with doing God's will; 10:10 names the offering of Jesus Christ's body.
The different setting and connected wording matter more than one noun alone.
Swete's reported combinations do not look like wholesale replacement of the
Psalm by the Hebrews quotation. Selective influence on one word nevertheless
remains possible; retained differences cannot disprove it.

## Competing histories and quotation limits

Van der Bergh's [complete 2008 article](https://repository.up.ac.za/handle/2263/9955),
printed 353–382, argues for original Greek body and an interpretive translation
of Hebrew ears. He separately reconstructs an earlier Greek text and Hebrews'
probable exemplar, allowing plural burnt offerings in the latter. His four
proposed authorial changes therefore depend on a reconstructed exemplar, not
an extant copy demonstrably used by the author. The article's broad rejection
of early ears is not adopted as established history.

The full institutional HTML text of Amphoux and Dorival's
[2006 counterargument](https://www.persee.fr/doc/mom_0151-7015_2006_mel_35_1_2442),
printed 315–327, was also read. They prefer original **ὦτα**, not Rahlfs'
**ὠτία**, and propose influence from Hebrews into the Psalter. Their Old Latin
and patristic evidence challenges manuscript-count arguments. Their precise
Alexandrian chronology depends on disputed dating, collection history and
transmission assumptions; it is not independently measured here. Bodmer XXIV's
body reading is reported by them, not newly verified in its edition or pixels.

Gäbel's 2024 chapter provides a newer, explicit quotation-control method.
Only its introduction/conventions and Irenaeus/Origen sections were consulted
here: printed 3–9 / preview PDF 21–27, with 7–9 visually inspected. This is
not a full-chapter consultation. It distinguishes actual Latin from proposed
Greek retroversion and identifies a purported early Psalm-body quotation in
Origen as possibly a quotation of Hebrews instead.

The actual [Latin text attributed to Harvey's edition](https://bibleox.com/la/la/w/LATIN-Adversus_haereses,_LIBER_IV/)
at Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 4.17.1, has **aures autem perfecisti mihi**.
Its context groups Samuel and Psalm testimony around obedience rather than
God's need for offerings. That establishes the consulted Latin reading, not
a surviving second-century Greek page. Gäbel treats the ears reading as secured
by the interpretation, while also noting the Latin Psalter relationship.
[Swete's own discussion](https://ccel.org/ccel/swete/greekot/greekot.v.iii.html)
raises possible Gallican correction. Root does not resolve that transmission
question: an obedience theme alone does not discriminate ears from a body
prepared for obedience. Neither quotation-counting nor automatic dismissal of
the Latin is justified.

## POB impact and next discriminating evidence

The present 40:6 note's unqualified attribution to “the Septuagint” obscures
the distinction between body-bearing Greek witnesses and an ears-printing
critical edition. A separate, reviewed note correction should explain that
difference and avoid presenting the direction of dependence as settled.
The main Psalm and Hebrews texts need not change merely to eliminate their
difference. Existing theological/revision fields also describe “dug” where the
active main English says “opened”; old reasoning is not a fresh endorsement.

For Greek priority, consult the actual Bodmer XXIV target edition/image and
the complete modern local apparatus, then evaluate Gäbel's remaining catalogue
and conclusion and the primary passages it cites. Distinguish Psalm quotations
from quotations of Hebrews, and Latin copying/revision from authorial Greek.
Do not repeat these completed article/selected-text readings as another vote.
For Hebrew English, review the concrete image and natural-English alternatives
separately before changing “opened.” No generated image can decide this
transmission history. This case supplies no new book or canon-addition argument.
