# Jeremiah 27 heading and ruler name comparison

Jeremiah 27:1 is a genuine source-text question, not just an English style choice. POB currently follows WLC and dates the heading to Jehoiakim. Published Hebrew variant reports and a selected Syriac text instead name Zedekiah; selected Greek texts lack this heading, while a Greek marginal report contains it with Jehoiakim. These alternatives could change the passage's historical setting. None establishes a newly discovered reading or the author's original wording.

The working proposal is to consider Zedekiah within the longer Hebrew form. Historical priority remains unresolved, and neither the canonical Hebrew nor POB English is changed in this comparison. The [machine record](../sources/textual_restoration/comparisons/jeremiah27_1_heading.2026-10-10.v1.json) preserves the proposal separately from the independent review and final editorial disposition.

## The three alternatives

| Alternative | Evidence worth comparing | Principal competing explanation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Longer heading with Jehoiakim | Received WLC text, selected Latin, and Swete's positive Greek marginal report | A name or heading may have entered from the similar Jeremiah 26:1 heading |
| Longer heading with Zedekiah | Published Hebrew text, initial-state and marginal reports; selected Syriac; chapter context | Contextual harmonization can produce the same agreement without preserving an earlier name |
| Shorter form without this heading | Continuous selected Greek text at the aligned boundary | Omission or revision remains possible; this is a different literary-form decision, not another royal-name spelling |

Jeremiah 27:3 and 27:12 name Zedekiah. Verse 20 presupposes the exile of Jeconiah, and 28:1 continues into Zedekiah's fourth year. This makes Zedekiah a plausible correction, but narrative fit is not proof of textual priority. An earlier oracle followed by later enactment, or a composite arrangement, remains a serious defense of the awkward received heading. Jeremiah 26:1 is closely similar, not verbatim identical: the reign noun differs and 27:1 includes the recipient Jeremiah.

## Hebrew evidence and physical preservation

[De Rossi's Variae lectiones volume III, printed pages 98–99](https://archive.org/details/VariaeLectiones/Variae%20Lectiones%203/page/n112/mode/2up) reports צדקיהו under the attribution “Kenn.224,590 marg.,primo154.” These are different reported states: text in 224, margin in 590, and an initial reading in 154. The acquisition agent visually inspected both complete printed pages. This establishes published Hebrew attestation; it is not a fresh reading of those manuscripts, a verified date for the marginal hand, or three independent early witnesses.

The [Kennicott manuscript key](https://dershowitz.net/kennicott) provisionally identifies 154 with Karlsruhe/Reuchlin 3 and 590 with Vienna NB 15. Its date for 590 differs from a commonly repeated early date. Body dates cannot date an individual marginal correction; 224's absence from this key is a coverage gap. No chronological preference is assigned from these mappings.

[Qumran Digital's 4Q72 fragment 31](https://lexicon.qumran-digital.org/transcriptions/4Q72/2026-05-21/index.html?v=2026-05-21) has four available diplomatic rows. In line 1, אמר is outside editorial supply before the next speech formula, while the preceding recipient and divine-name phrase is supplied. Neither ruler nor date survives in those rows. This is limited evidence for a heading end or lead-in, not preservation of the complete received heading and not support for either king. The parallel pointed reference is not fragment ink. The private legacy and public transcriptions share related ancestry and are not independent manuscript votes.

The local discovery query initially used the wrong book label. It was discarded and rerun with `Jer`, validated by the positive control `Jer 42:7 → 2Q13`. Correctly indexed results associate 27:1–3 with 4Q72. A missing tag elsewhere is not an attested omission.

## Syriac Greek and Latin controls

The [CAL Syriac chapter 27](https://cal.huc.edu/get_a_chapter.php?file=62013&sub=27&cset=Syriac) names Zedekiah at 27:1, 3 and 12. Its [text information](https://cal.huc.edu/get_file_info.php?coord=62013) identifies a Leiden-derived main text with some corrections from manuscript 7a1 and apparatus variants. This is a selected electronic text, not a single physical manuscript or a finding of Syriac unanimity. De Rossi's Syriac agreement report is not a second fresh collation.

The publisher's Rahlfs–Hanhart Greek [chapter 34](https://www.die-bibel.de/bibel/LXX/JER.34), checked with complete adjacent chapters 33 and 35, begins with the yoke instructions corresponding to Hebrew 27:2. The heading is absent from that selected boundary. Swete's continuous text does the same, but labels those instructions 34:1 rather than the publisher's 34:2. Align by content, not bare verse numbers.

[Swete volume III, printed page 302](https://archive.org/download/theoldtestamenti03swetuoft_202003/theoldtestamenti03swetuoft_jp2.zip/theoldtestamenti03swetuoft_jp2%2Ftheoldtestamenti03swetuoft_0325.jp2) positively reports a prefixed Jehoiakim heading in `Qmg`, with an Aquila label and asterisk. The pertinent preface identifies Q as Codex Marchalianus and discusses its marginal material; the local `Qmg` entry does not identify a particular hand. This defeats an “all Greek witnesses omit” claim. It does not establish a separately preserved Aquila manuscript or the age of this individual marginal reading.

The selected Weber–Gryson [Latin chapter 27](https://www.die-bibel.de/bibel/VUL/JER.27) retains Jehoiakim in verse 1 and Zedekiah in verses 3 and 12. Versional readings require analysis of translation and correction history; counting selected editions as independent Hebrew manuscripts would overstate the evidence.

## Editorial decision and the next useful evidence

The root proposal assigns moderate working preference to Zedekiah as a correction within the longer form, not as a demonstrated earliest reading. One independent source critic assesses that proposal. Disagreement is preserved; review is not repeated until an agent agrees. The review result and final disposition are recorded separately so the original proposal remains auditable.

A source change requires a complete critical-source record and matching English candidate, explicit editorial pointing rather than falsely attributed manuscript accents, preserved prior history, a correct edition label, and source-selection, schema and export checks. No such candidate has been constructed here. Deleting the heading and correcting its name are mutually distinct decisions and must not be silently combined.

The next discriminating work is verification of the reported Hebrew manuscript states and hands, a modern local apparatus, the Syriac variant basis, or the Greek local marginal evidence and translation practice. Repeating the same preference poll adds no evidence. Existing misanchored notes and legacy divine-name metadata are separate defects; fixing them would not answer the source-priority question.

This case contributes a bounded, reproducible comparison of a meaning-bearing variant. It does not prove comprehensive OT or NT coverage, novel manuscript discovery, recovered autographs, or any change to the biblical canon. Canon evaluation remains a separate historical and community question; manuscript preservation alone cannot establish canonical authority.
