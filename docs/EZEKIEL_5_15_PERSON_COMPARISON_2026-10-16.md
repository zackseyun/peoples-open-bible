# Ezekiel 5 15 grammatical person and English address

**Retain the declared Hebrew provisionally and keep English “you shall
become,” but disclose the grammatical shift.** The Greek supports second-person
address; the Qumran spelling is real evidence, but its grammatical interpretation
is disputed. This comparison does not establish a second-person Hebrew original.

## What the witnesses establish

| Evidence | Reading and interpretation | Limit |
|---|---|---|

It must not silently become והיית or a fully vocalized second-feminine form. |
| [Swete volume III](https://archive.org/download/theoldtestamenti03swetuoft_202003/theoldtestamenti03swetuoft.pdf), third edition, 1905, printed p. 393 / PDF p. 417 | καὶ ἔσῃ, “and you shall be,” in the selected Greek text based here on Vaticanus | The Greek verb marks second singular, not gender. A different Hebrew reading and translational smoothing both remain possible. No claim of unanimity across Greek manuscripts follows from this selected text. |

The complete published 11Q4 lines around the verb were inspected. The opening
supply closes before the verb, and later gaps and uncertain letters remain
marked. In the same row, the following noun material is only partly preserved;
it cannot become an entirely legible phrase merely because the verb is clear.
The Greek page also differs from the Hebrew in its following description and
judgment wording. Agreement on person does not establish agreement on the
whole verse or justify splicing the two literary forms.

## The published disagreement matters

[Fabry's chapter](https://doi.org/10.1515/9783110624250-001), printed p. 12,
interprets the Qumran form as second feminine and recommends the second-person
reading proposed in the apparatus he cites. His argument directly supports a
change, not just retaining the current base. The accessible
[publisher preview](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9783110624250_A39059118/preview-9783110624250_A39059118.pdf)
contains this discussion.

However, [Patmore's thesis](https://etheses.durham.ac.uk/id/eprint/2381/1/2381_391.pdf),
printed p. 36 / PDF p. 41, reports Herbert's objection: the short spelling could
be erroneous and reflect either second masculine or third feminine singular.
Notes 90 and 92 point to Herbert's DJD XXIII p. 22. The complete thesis page
was visually read here; this is direct consultation of Patmore's report, not
of Herbert's original page. That distinction also prevents Patmore and Herbert
from becoming two independent witnesses.

The competing analyses cannot be settled by expanding the four letters into
the form one expects. A second-person interpretation must explain the spelling;
a third-feminine interpretation must explain the missing final he. Counting
one possible missing letter on either side is not a transmission argument.
Before selecting a changed Hebrew text, inspect Herbert's discussion, the
relevant plate and the scribe's actually preserved comparable forms. Restored
forms elsewhere in the transcription cannot establish the scribe's habits.

## Grammar and the source decision

Second person fits the direct address in 5:14 and the second-person suffixes
later in 5:15. This is the strongest contextual argument for changing the
Hebrew, and the Greek adds early versional support. The strongest objection
is that Hebrew prophetic discourse can change grammatical person while retaining
the same referent. [GKC section 144p](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Gesenius%27_Hebrew_Grammar/144._Peculiarities_in_the_Representation_of_the_Subject_%28especially_in_the_Verbal-clause%29)
recognizes such shifts, while qualifying some examples as textually disputed.
The grammatical possibility does not establish this verse's transmission history.

The [UBS Ezekiel handbook discussion](https://tips.translation.bible/story/translation-commentary-on-ezekiel-515/)
explicitly treats either a copying error or a legitimate person shift as
possible at this verse. It recommends English second person under either
explanation. This is useful evidence that English “you” need not imply a
source emendation, not authority to skip our own source decision.

[NET's textual note 1](https://classic.net.bible.org/verse.php?book=Eze&chapter=5&verse=15)
also proposes a consonantal analysis in which final he can serve as a vowel
letter with a second-masculine perfect. That is not the third-feminine analysis
of the retained WLC pointing, nor does it establish second-feminine morphology.
The note's version and scroll claims are not independent collations of each
version by POB. The modern English versions displayed on that page supply no
manuscript votes and are not used to draft a new main translation.

Under the retained source, Jerusalem is a plausible feminine referent. The
nearby שפטים is masculine plural and does not straightforwardly account for
the verb's feminine-singular agreement. An abstract outcome interpretation
would need its own argument; it must not be smuggled in as grammatical agreement.

The older direct-language witness makes the second-person proposal worth
serious consideration, but does not disambiguate its own short spelling.
Removing the modest chronological preference would not change the present
outcome: source priority remains unresolved, not a demonstrated Masoretic
victory. Retention is provisional; the next discriminating evidence is
orthographic and grammatical, not another vote on the same letters.

## English and reader corrections

Keeping “you shall become” maintains the address to Jerusalem. Its cost is
smoothing the Hebrew third-person form; “she/it shall become” would expose that
form but require readers to follow the referent shift. This comparison supports
retention with disclosure, not a newly proven optimal rendering or a blind
English-preference result.

The current explanation wrongly conflates the referent's feminine gender with
grammatical person. The scoped application should replace that rationale and
add a reader note explaining the Hebrew third person, Greek second person and
disputed Qumran form. Three existing note anchors belong at “warning,” “horror”
and “wrathful rebukes,” not “taunt” or “Yahweh.” A stale lexical entry saying
the main text uses “the LORD” should match the already present “Yahweh”; this
is metadata reconciliation, not a new divine-name policy.

The candidate retains every source character and all marker-free main English.
Before application, freeze its complete record, archive the old review fields
without transferring approval, check the notes and source claims independently,
and compare the actual full-book export. The neighboring verses and the
Greek/Hebrew noun-list difference are outside this correction, not certified.
