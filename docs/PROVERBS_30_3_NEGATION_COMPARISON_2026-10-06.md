# Proverbs 30 3 negation scope comparison

Provisionally retain POB's negative knowledge claim. The positive interpretation
is grammatically possible and meaningfully different, but an unrepeated negative
does not make POB's rendering erroneous. This comparison resolves the immediate
lead from [30:1](PROVERBS_30_1_SOURCE_COMPARISON_2026-10-06.md) without applying
a new source, English or note. It does not establish earliest wording or a novel

## Hebrew syntax and opposing interpretations

The selected text has one explicit negative: וְלֹא לָמַדְתִּי חָכְמָה,
followed by וְדַעַת קְדֹשִׁים אֵדָע. POB interprets the latter as negative,
continuing the confession of lacking wisdom. A positive reading instead
contrasts acquired wisdom with knowledge the speaker nevertheless possesses.
Both preserve consonants, vowels and word division; the difference is negative
scope and discourse interpretation, not restoration of a lost לֹא.

[GKC 152z](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Gesenius%27_Hebrew_Grammar/152._Negative_Sentences#GHGpar-152-z)
permits a negative in the first clause to govern a parallel second clause,
with or without waw. Root and the read-only Hebrew analyst inspected its actual
body. Three checked Hebrew controls make that possibility concrete:

| Control in selected Hebrew | Relevant feature |
| --- | --- |
| Psalm 9:19 MT / 9:18 English | No repeated negative in the second clause; inheritance crosses an athnach. |
| Psalm 44:19 MT / 44:18 English | A different verbal form in the second clause does not by itself end negative scope. |
| Isaiah 38:18 | The death/praise clause follows the negative Sheol/thank clause without its own negative. |

These controls defeat an automatic positive inference; they do not force a
negative in Proverbs. The strongest local negative argument is synonymous
parallelism: verse 2 denies human understanding; verse 3 links learning wisdom
with knowing the sacred knowledge; verse 4 asks questions about extraordinary
knowledge and acts. Proverbs 9:10 places the exact knowledge phrase alongside
wisdom and identifies it with understanding. That same-book relationship favors
continuation of the confession, although it does not exclude a literary contrast.

The strongest positive argument distinguishes human training from revelatory
knowledge. The second clause fronts its object and changes from perfect to
imperfect; verses 5–9 include assertions and prayer compatible with religious
knowledge. Numbers 24:16 positively combines knowing with knowledge of the
Most High in an oracle, offering a real literary comparison. Nevertheless,
fronting fits poetic parallelism too, and the form change does not encode an
unambiguous adversative. Lack of human understanding need not exclude religious
knowledge; conversely, affirming God's word need not imply a claim to master
the extraordinary knowledge invoked by verse 4. Neither argument is decisive.

## Greek and scholarly argument

The pinned CATSS/Rahlfs-derived Greek line 722 makes a positive knowledge claim.
It also positively credits God with teaching wisdom in the first clause, rather
than preserving the selected Hebrew's negative learning clause. It therefore
supports a distinct ancient interpretation of this verse, not a clean vote for
one scope assignment in the unchanged Hebrew. Translation technique or a
different Vorlage remains unresolved. No Hebrew negative is deleted or inserted
on the strength of this edition-derived control; Greek unanimity is not claimed.

[McKenzie and Shelton 2020](https://www.sebts.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/McKenzie.pdf),
printed pages 12–14 and note 28, advocates a positive knowledge contrast while
allowing negative ellipsis. Its argument draws on the oracle framework, Numbers
24 and the following questions. Its reports about Fox's changing interpretation
and Moore's proposal are mediated citations, not independently inspected works.
The article's larger son-identity and canonical-order thesis is not adopted as
proof of local polarity. Complete relevant native pages and footnotes were
visually checked; no new English draft was prepared from its embedded translations.
