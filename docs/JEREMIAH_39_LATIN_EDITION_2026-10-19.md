# Jeremiah 39 Latin edition controls

Ranke's original printed transcription now directly confirms the shorter
Latin sequence previously checked through Bogaert's report. The transitions
38:28 → 39:3 → 39:14 occur within one transcribed column, not across missing
printed leaves. This strengthens Latin attestation, not Hebrew priority.
Retain POB's longer source and English provisionally; no note, approval,
reader-layout or canon change follows.

## What the original edition shows

[Ranke, 1871, printed p. 93](https://books.google.com/books?id=9bNCAQAAMAAJ&pg=PA93),
column B, continues the preceding conversation, then Jeremiah's remaining in
custody until Jerusalem's capture. The officers enter and sit at the middle
gate; they then send for Jeremiah and entrust him to Gedaliah. Neither
39:1–2 nor 4–13 appears between those clauses. There is no printed lacuna
at either transition. The footer identifies the column's coverage as
38:27–39:14. A coverage range alone would not prove continuity; the actual
column supplies it.

The [diplomatic commentary, p. 154](https://books.google.com/books?id=9bNCAQAAMAAJ&pg=PA154),
note 93 B10, explicitly says the codex lacks 39:1–2. The 3 → 14 transition
is independently visible in the transcription, even though this note does
not separately enumerate 4–13. These are two ways the same edition describes
one manuscript, not additional ancient witnesses.

[Printed p. 94](https://books.google.com/books?id=9bNCAQAAMAAJ&pg=PA94),
column A, continues verse 14's dwelling among the people, then has four
dotted lines before the Ebed-melech oracle. The p. 154 note 94 A2–5 says
these lines appear to have been written in minium. They do not provide a
Latin reading of verse 15. Another three dotted lines at A22–24 receive
the same minium qualification; they are not silently restored here either.
The next release account begins at the bottom of A and continues in B;
chapter 40 is not absent from the Latin edition.

## The uncertain ending of verse 14

The transcription's last line on p. 93 ends `IAFRET`. In note 93 B25, Ranke
explains that he printed the alternative he then preferred, but says a
subsequent reinspection of the codex yielded `TAFRET`. This refines the
[preceding comparison](JEREMIAH_39_RELEASE_FORMS_2026-10-19.md): “proposal”
alone understates his reported reinspection. It remains his historical
reading claim, not our inspection of manuscript ink. The geographical
interpretation and an exact underlying Greek or Hebrew expression are not
established by these letters. Neither form is adopted into POB.

## Consequence and next evidence

The shorter Latin form is now supported by a directly consulted original
edition, rather than only a later quotation. It remains compatible with
several histories: an inherited short Greek form, Greek-stage loss or revision,
or Latin-stage changes. The earlier Hebrew repeated-ending countermodel for
4–13 does not by itself explain the extra Latin absence of 1–2. Ranke's
commentary verifies the minus, not its date or cause. Nor does the number of
modern scholars repeating it provide independent ancestry.
