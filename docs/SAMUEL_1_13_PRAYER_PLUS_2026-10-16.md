# First Samuel 1 13 and the hearing of Hannah

## Decision

The Greek clause meaning **“and the Lord heard her”** is a consequential
published variant, not new manuscript ink. It places divine hearing before
Eli's mistaken judgment and subsequent blessing. POB's current WLC-based text
does not contain it. Retain that source and English provisionally while testing
the clause's history; neither earlier Greek priority nor an exact lost Hebrew
sentence has been established by this comparison.

The subsequent 4Q51 check adds qualified early Hebrew support for the shorter
junction. Retention is therefore supported by a local manuscript reading, not
just by the received text; original priority remains unresolved.

This is a control on the [Samuel narrative-form investigation](SAMUEL_17_18_NARRATIVE_FORMS_2026-10-16.md):
the B-based edition is a witness to evaluate, not an automatic definition of
the original Greek. The findings here do not settle manuscript 845's omissions
or justify transferring a priority decision between passages.

## What the printed apparatus attests

Brooke, McLean and Thackeray's [Samuel edition](https://tmcdaniel.palmerseminary.edu/Brooke%26McLean/LXX_Brooke%26McLean_2-1.pdf),
printed p.3 / PDF19, places the addition after the unheard-voice verb:
`και εισηκουσεν αυτης κ̅ς̅`. Its positive Greek support is
**M N aᵇ b d–g m–t w z b₂ c₂ e₂**, with **⟨236⟩** separately reported.
The B-based main text lacks it.

This is not a list of identical readings. The correction aᵇ is not the first
hand. Samuel's preface defines b as b′ plus italic b; b′ has a different verb
form inside the entry. Other witnesses vary order, pronoun case and the article;
Chr has “God,” and ⟨236⟩ omits “Lord.” The angle-bracket report and patristic
citation must not become newly inspected continuous-text manuscripts. Root and
a second reader inspected the complete page and enlarged entry; the second
reader checked the supporting sigla conventions. No manuscript photograph was
transcribed for this case.

## The proposed earlier Hebrew and a countercheck

In [Aejmelaeus 2020](https://publicera.kb.se/sea/article/download/15079/12016/27520),
*New Perspectives on the Textual Character of Codex Vaticanus*, pp.13–15,
the author selects the longer Greek reading and proposes earlier Hebrew
behind it. She links the clause to Rachel's story in Genesis 30:22, argues
that different Greek wording requires Hebrew-stage borrowing, and suggests
that later Hebrew editing diminished Hannah's role. Her wider account treats
some Vaticanus agreements with MT as revisions rather than original Greek.
These are historical arguments, not an additional Hebrew manuscript.
Root read the complete indexed article text and notes, pp.1–21. This is not
a claim of full visual verification of its printed apparatus. The separate
2019 study has subsequently been read as described below.

The actual [Genesis apparatus](https://tmcdaniel.palmerseminary.edu/Brooke%26McLean/LXX_Brooke%26McLean_1-1.pdf),
printed p.81 / PDF97, supplies two limited controls:

- At **30:17**, the printed hearing verb has an alternative `εισηκουσεν`
  in **i** and **s(mg)**. The latter is marginal support.
- At **30:22**, **Cyr** has “Lord” for the second “God,” in the hearing
  clause—not the first “God” in the remembrance clause. This is reported
  patristic evidence.

Both readers checked the full page and enlarged entries. These variants are
at different locations and in different witnesses: combining them would
invent a Genesis sentence. They do not prove that Samuel borrowed in Greek.
They do show why nonidentity with one selected Greek Genesis reading cannot,
by itself, exclude Greek-stage adaptation. A Hebrew-stage explanation remains
possible; the linguistic contrast does not independently establish it.

## Early Hebrew evidence at the disputed junction

The [versioned Qumran-Digital transcription](https://lexicon.qumran-digital.org/transcriptions/4Q51/2025-03-11/index.html)
of 4Q51, fragment 1b line 6, prints the sequence `ע֯ ויח` without a
reconstruction gap between these remains. Its surrounding supplied text
identifies the ayin as the end of **ישמע**, followed immediately by the
beginning of **ויחשבה**. The Greek divine-hearing clause would intervene
between those words. The transcription's licence is CC-BY-SA 4.0; this short
excerpt retains its uncertainty mark.

[DJD XVII](https://messianicessenes.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/07/djd-17.pdf),
printed pp.29–30 / PDF52–53, agrees at column I fragment b line 6. Its reading
note expressly limits the first letter to the merest surviving ayin tip. Most
of the preceding clause and the completion of the following verb are supplied.
Root visually checked both complete pages and Plate I / PDF296. The available
plate scan is too coarse for a fresh independent decipherment of that tiny
trace. A second reader checked the edition's junction and preservation limits,
not the plate. These two editions concern one manuscript, not two witnesses.

**Consequence:** accepting the published trace identification and placement,
4Q51 supports the short Hebrew junction at the actual insertion site. It is
not merely evidence that the manuscript contains Samuel. This makes an account
in which the short sequence is only a late Masoretic development untenable,
but does not refute an earlier omission followed by Greek revision. Nor does
it establish Greek expansion, exclude every other placement of extra material,
or prove that a longer Hebrew form never existed. The larger chapter argument
must explain this local short Hebrew evidence as well as the Greek plus.

## Narrative consequence and competing explanations

The local WLC control preserves Eli's blessing in 1:17, divine remembrance
of Hannah in 1:19, her naming explanation in 1:20, and her explicit account of
answered prayer in 1:27. The shorter 1:13 therefore does not erase her prayer
or its efficacy. An earlier divine-hearing clause would change the timing and
the narrator's explicit assurance, not introduce answered prayer into a story
otherwise lacking it. This contextual observation does not disprove a more
selective editorial tendency.

Two explanations remain open: an older longer text followed by omission and
Hebrew-directed Greek revision, or a Greek explanatory expansion affirming
that inaudible prayer was nevertheless heard. A broader Hebrew reshaping
argument must account for the retained material as well as the missing clause.
Neither widespread support nor narrative attractiveness alone selects a
history. No Hebrew retroversion is promoted into the source corpus, and no
English or footnote application is made in this pass.

## Chapter argument and the mixed Qumran witness

The complete indexed text of [Aejmelaeus 2019](https://www.academia.edu/62201820/Was_Samuel_Meant_to_Be_a_Nazirite_The_First_Chapter_of_Samuel_and_the_Paradigm_Shift_in_Textual_Study_of_the_Hebrew_Bible),
*Textus* 28:1–20, including its 56 notes, has now been read. This is not a
downloaded PDF or visual certification of its extracted tables. Her argument
links changes surrounding Hannah's vow to legal concerns and proposes a
first-century-CE-or-later reworking (pp.13–17). She acknowledges retained
before-the-Lord expressions and the story's happy outcome. Her proposal is
selective editing, not wholesale removal of Hannah's prayer.

DJD XVII p.5 dates the script of 4Q51 to approximately 50–25 BCE. This is the
edition's palaeographic estimate, not a new radiocarbon determination. Combined
with the qualified short junction above, it rules against that junction first
arising in the proposed later campaign. A later editor could have inherited
an already short text; an independent earlier omission remains possible.

The same manuscript preserves the Nazirite designation at 1:22 (DJD p.31,
column II line 3). Short 1:13 and explicit Nazirite 1:22 therefore coexist:
the features do not require one indivisible longer-versus-shorter edition.
The designation at 1:11 is supplied in reconstruction, not surviving ink.
Josephus's later knowledge of a longer form cannot by itself date the first
existence of a shorter one. These are constraints on the proposed history,
not disproof of all purposeful editing or proof of original short readings.
