# Psalm 151: compare literary forms, do not manufacture a hybrid

Retain POB’s Greek-based source and English provisionally. The related Hebrew
in 11Q5 supplies consequential comparison material, but is not demonstrably
the exact Hebrew exemplar of our Greek text. Publish the relationship as a
comparison, not a reconstructed original or a new canon decision.

The [machine record](../sources/textual_restoration/comparisons/psalm151_literary_forms.2026-10-06.v1.json)
pins all seven POB verses, their source files and the acquired controls. This
is a published-source comparison, not a new reading of ancient ink.

## What actually differs

Root read all seven Greek/POB verses and the published Hebrew at 11Q5 XXVIII
3–14. The alignment follows content, not identical verse numbers:

| Greek Psalm 151 | Hebrew 11Q5 XXVIII | Material difference or preservation limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1, youth and sheep | 151A, 3–4 | Hebrew also has David governing goats. |
| 2, instruments | 151A, 4–5 | Hebrew continues with giving glory to Yahweh. |
| 3, reporting and hearing | 151A, 5–8 | Hebrew has a longer nature/speech passage; some readings and segmentation are disputed. |
| 4, messenger and anointing | 151A, 8–11 | Hebrew names Samuel and continues into David’s appointment. |
| 5, brothers rejected | 151A, 9–10 | Hebrew expands their appearance and places their rejection before the concluding anointing. |
| No equivalent leadership ending | 151A, 11–12 | Hebrew ends with David as leader of God’s people and ruler among the covenant’s sons. |
| 6–7, encounter and victory | 151B, 13–14 | Only the opening of the second Hebrew composition survives here. The Greek beheading and removal of disgrace have no extant counterpart in these lines. |

The Samuel identification is unusually useful. The bounded published phrase
שלח נביאו למושחני את שמואל supports the research gloss “he sent his prophet
to anoint me—Samuel.” Greek verse 4 says his messenger, without that name.
POB’s existing “messenger” is defensible; inserting “Samuel” into the Greek
translation would add an identification not expressed there. Hebrew comparison
supports a human-messenger interpretation but does not prove that a Greek
reader could never understand an angelic messenger.

The two endings change the emphasis: covenant leadership in Hebrew 151A,
victory and removal of Israel’s disgrace in Greek 151. Neither is a newly
discovered reading. Nor may the missing Hebrew battle ending be filled with
Greek verses 6–7 and described as recovered Hebrew.

## Source controls and limits

[Qumran-Digital’s 11Q5 transcription](https://lexicon.qumran-digital.org/transcriptions/11Q5/2026-05-21/index.html?v=2026-05-21)
is a modern edition drawing on Abegg and subsequent editorial work, not a
second physical scroll. Its [FAQ](https://lexicon.qumran-digital.org/faq/v1/en/index.html)
warns that working variants/comments need independent verification. At 28:6,
parenthetical alternatives are modern proposals, not two ancient hands.
The erased letter at 28:7 and above-line ומושל, “and ruler,” at 28:11 are
different phenomena; the latter contributes to the leadership ending.
At 28:13–14, uncertain/restored letters, alternative title readings and gaps
remain marked. No confident new translation of the disputed nature passage
or missing battle narrative is adopted. Credit Qumran-Digital/Qumran-Wörterbuch
and the Abegg baseline; the upstream transcription is CC BY-SA 4.0. Only the
short attributed excerpt and our comparison are included, not a relicensed
transcription corpus. The full acquired HTML stays temporary.

Swete’s apparatus is selective. B is the selected Vaticanus text here; ℵ is
Sinaiticus, A Alexandrinus, R the Verona Greek/Latin Psalter and T the Zurich
Psalter. Crucially, the preface reports Psalm 151 in R as a later-hand addition,
not evidence of its presence in the original sixth-century codex. Edition
reports and digital copies must not become extra manuscript votes.

## A consequential Greek variant, still unresolved

At verse 4 the printed apparatus reports `ελαιω] ελεει ℵAR`: oil versus mercy.
This could yield “anointed me with the mercy of his anointing,” rather than
POB’s oil. It is a genuine meaning-changing alternative, not an English synonym.
Hebrew 151A explicitly has holy oil, making oil a natural contextual reading.
But that related literary form cannot alone settle the earliest Greek wording.
The strongest countercase is substantial Greek support for mercy; oil could
also be a contextual normalization. Similar-sounding Greek forms permit a
copying hypothesis, not proof of its direction. Retain oil provisionally in
the declared selected source, without awarding it historical priority. A
modern passage apparatus and hand-separated Greek collation are the next
discriminating evidence, not more model agreement. R’s later addition cannot
be dated from the age of its host codex. Chronological preference alone does
not decide this unit.

## Competing histories

[Andrew C. Witt, JESOT 3.1 (2014), 77–97](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/340794094_David_the_%27Ruler_of_the_Sons_of_his_Covenant%27_mwsl_bbny_brytw_The_Expansion_of_Psalm_151A_in_11QPsa)
argues for Hebrew expansion using literary framing and covenant-community
vocabulary. Root consulted its displayed comparison and argument, pp. 85–97;
the [author’s institutional bibliography](https://www.tyndale.ca/publications/david-the-ruler-the-sons-his-covenant-mwsl-bbny-brytw-the-expansion-psalm-151a-in)
confirms the journal. His account of other scholars is mediated, not our direct
consultation of their work. Three histories remain live: Greek abbreviation/
combination of longer Hebrew; Qumran expansion of a shorter predecessor;
or separate development from a shared lost predecessor. The vocabulary
argument is suggestive, not decisive: compatible community language does not
date a change, and coherent framing could be abbreviated as well as expanded.
