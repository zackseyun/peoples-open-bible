# First Samuel 15 29 source comparison

## Result and consequence for POB

This passage contains three separate textual questions. Published 4Q51 supplies
direct Hebrew support for **ישוב**, “turn back,” against WLC **ישקר**, “lie.”
It does not preserve the proposed opening about Israel's division. The longer
Greek ending is another question again: establishing an early Greek clause
would not establish its exact Hebrew ancestor.

The [evidence record](../sources/textual_restoration/comparisons/samuel15_29.2026-10-16.v1.json)
registers the Hebrew alternative as an attested source candidate, not merely
another English gloss. POB's Hebrew, main English and notes remain unchanged
provisionally. No earliest-wording judgment, fresh decipherment or whole-verse
approval follows. The previous 14:41 disclosure remains intact.

## Greek ending and the Numbers comparison

Aejmelaeus's [2022 chapter](https://researchportal.helsinki.fi/en/publications/challenges-in-preparing-the-critical-edition-of-1-samuel/),
author pp. 10–11 / PDF pp. 11–12, selects the longer ending as Old Greek and
explains the isolated pronoun as residue of shortening toward Hebrew. Her
proposed Hebrew is explicitly a retroversion. Yardney accepts that Greek
selection; she is not an independent opposing witness against the ending.
The interrogative force is an interpretation, not a recovered Hebrew question.

The direct [Numbers 23:19 Greek control](https://www.die-bibel.de/bibel/LXX/NUM.23)
has passive threatening vocabulary earlier and a rhetorical final **ἐμμενεῖ**
question. The longer Samuel clause uses an active threatening verb and the
same final verb, but is not a verbatim copy of the whole Numbers sentence.
Local WLC Numbers instead has saying, doing, speaking and establishing.
This makes a Greek-level literary relationship a concrete possibility;
it does not prove Greek expansion or exclude a Hebrew antecedent. The shared
reliability theme alone cannot determine the direction or language of borrowing.

## Augustine quotes and interprets the long clause

Hoffmann's CSEL edition of *De civitate Dei* 17.7 prints an explicit Latin
quotation, **ipse minatur, et non permanet**, after the nonhuman comparison
(printed p. 229, lines 9–12). Later Augustine repeats it and assigns the action
to humanity, not God (p. 231, lines 3–6). This is a substantive longer-clause
parallel, stronger than Josephus's shared reliability vocabulary. It is not
evidence that the clause was always understood as a rhetorical question about
God. Latin tense and this interpretation do not select the Greek tense or
recover its punctuation. The chapter also quotes the Israel-division opening;
that is Latin reception evidence, not preserved Hebrew support for 4Q51's
supplied opening.

The original-language check uses the [OpenGreekAndLatin electronic edition](https://github.com/OpenGreekAndLatin/csel-dev/blob/a930441f99e57a62724991a91d102bf2053635c7/data/stoa0040/stoa003/stoa0040.stoa003.opp-lat3.xml)
and the [underlying public-domain scan](https://archive.org/details/corpusscriptoru07wiengoog/page/n245/mode/2up).
Root read the entire chapter and its notes in XML, then visually checked complete
printed pp. 228–232 / PDF pp. 245–249. A delegated reader independently read the
complete XML chapter and notes, not the scan. The XML joins both CSEL volumes
under opp-lat3; its old web index's separate opp-lat4 download no longer exists.
Neither presentation is another ancient witness, and the repeated quotation
within the chapter is not an independent vote. These are edited author-text
readings, not new collation of a Latin Bible manuscript; the ancient author's
period must not be assigned to the modern edition or an unidentified copy.

Two nearby statements need especially narrow attribution. Augustine's appeal
to Greek copies at p. 229, lines 21–25 concerns **regnum ab Israel** in verse 28,
not demonstrated consultation of the Greek ending in verse 29. Hoffmann's
apparatus at line 11 says the ending's words are absent from LXX; that is the
modern editor's note, not Augustine's claim or a complete inventory of Greek
evidence. BMT's explicit long-clause entry prevents treating it as universal
absence. The quotation establishes Latin attestation and an ancient alternative
interpretation, not earliest Greek priority or an exact Hebrew ancestor.

## Josephus and the reliability statement

Direct comparison with [Perseus's Niese-based Greek text](https://github.com/PerseusDL/canonical-greekLit/blob/03952c9751d7f963fe5cbbc062fbe2503181de47/data/tlg0526/tlg001/tlg0526.tlg001.perseus-grc2.xml)
now replaces the secondhand Josephus lead. In *Antiquities* 6.152–154, the
cloak tears, Samuel announces the transfer of kingship, and Saul asks for
public honor. Within that sequence, 6.153 says that God stands by the decisions
concerning Saul: **ἐμμένειν γὰρ τὸν θεὸν**. It then contrasts human changing
of mind with divine power. The reliability statement precedes that contrast;
the longer Samuel Greek puts its disputed threatening-and-standing clause
after the nonhuman comparison.

The shared **ἐμμένω** vocabulary makes the proposed echo concrete, but Josephus
does not reproduce the threat verb, the negative rhetorical question or the
long ending's full wording. The shorter Samuel text already conveys divine
constancy, so Josephus could also have expressed that theme without that
ending. His own narrative uses **ἐμμένειν** again at 6.205, of Saul not abiding
by what had happened. Lexical overlap alone is therefore not a diagnostic
quotation. The retelling is compatible with knowledge of the longer Greek,
but does not independently establish its priority, exact wording or Hebrew
ancestry. Nor does it decide **ישוב** versus **ישקר** or preserve the disputed
Israel-division opening.

The consulted source is an electronic edited text, not a Josephus manuscript
or newly checked critical apparatus. Its editorial deletion of **αὐτοῦ** in
6.153 must not be silently counted as accepted continuous text. Root read
6.130–160 in context and 6.205 as a lexical control; a search for this exact
infinitive is not a complete lemma concordance. This follow-up changes the
evidence classification from a reported parallel to a directly checked,
non-diagnostic parallel. POB's source and translation remain unchanged.

## Wellhausen and the Hebrew verb

Wellhausen's [1871 original](https://archive.org/download/dertextderbche00well/dertextderbche00well.pdf),
printed p. 101 / PDF p. 121, explicitly calls the Greek's **ישוב** in place of
**ישקר** a euphemism. His reference back to 15:1, printed p. 96 / PDF p. 116,
discusses avoidance of “God's voice” as an anthropomorphism. This establishes
his proposed theological motivation, not a paleographic chain or a manuscript
demonstration that **ישקר** came first. Both complete pages were visually read
by root and the delegated source reader.

The argument predates 4Q51 and cannot account for its Hebrew evidence directly.
A change made only by a Greek translator would not by itself explain surviving
Hebrew **ישוב**. A Hebrew-stage euphemistic alteration remains possible, however;
the scroll's agreement with Greek does not prove that their shared wording is
earliest. Neither the theological motivation nor the attestation alone selects
POB's source text. The result remains **HOLD** between the two Hebrew readings.

McCarter's staged-corruption explanation remains known here through Yardney,
not direct consultation of his p. 264. A bounded Google Books route did not
deliver that page, and the publisher's page supplied no target excerpt. These
access results are not evidence for or against his explanation.
