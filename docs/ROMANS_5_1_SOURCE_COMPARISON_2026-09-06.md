## Competing arguments

[Wallace's 2004 argument](https://bible.org/article/do-christians-have-peace-god-brief-examination-textual-problem-romans-51)
acknowledges stronger external support for the subjunctive but favors the
indicative through context and his assessment of 0220. He questions interpreting
the exhortation as enjoyment of already possessed peace. His proposed Tertius
error followed by Pauline correction is explicitly speculative; this project
does not treat it as observed history or a required explanation. His stronger
confidence in 0220 does not remove the later THGNT apparatus qualifier.

In [Head's 2006 post and named discussion](https://evangelicaltextualcriticism.blogspot.com/2006/02/0220-at-romans-51.html),
the fragment's hole and photographic limitations matter. Williams favors omega
interpreted indicatively; Holmes distinguishes written form from intended grammar.
Head defends an actual exhortation and warns against substituting what Paul
should have written. Heide points to a possible hortatory sequence in 5:1–3.
These are scholarly arguments, not a recovered autograph or measured image result.
Moir, Fee and the other works cited within that discussion were not separately read.

[Hsieh's 2025 excerpt](https://www.nelsonhsieh.com/p/romans-51-in-the-tyndale-house-gnt)
helped locate that earlier discussion. Its speculation about a future ECM
decision is not an actual ECM reading or a verified publication schedule.
No ECM, full patristic/lectionary apparatus or versional collation was acquired.

## Context and English consequence

The completed access and standing in 5:2 (ἐσχήκαμεν, ἑστήκαμεν), reconciliation
in 5:10, and reception in 5:11 favor “we have” as an introduction to stated
benefits. The contrary argument is substantial: the accomplished justification
can introduce an exhortation to exercise or maintain peace. Καυχώμεθα in 5:2–3
can be indicative or subjunctive, so treating the existing English “we boast”
as independent proof would be circular. No doctrine rules out an exhortation.

Spelling selection and intended mood therefore remain separate questions.
Do not silently turn a manuscript omega into an omicron, nor assume an omega
necessarily proves a different authorial intention. Greek source retention
here is provisional, not a claim that the surviving evidence decisively selects it.
