# Genesis 11 genealogy and age systems

## Decision

Genesis 11 requires comparison of genealogical structure as well as ages.
The selected Greek editions contain an additional Cainan between Arpachshad
and Shelah. The consulted Samaritan edition lacks that generation but agrees
with the selected Greek begetting ages for every shared figure. It also
supplies explicit lifetime totals absent from the Masoretic and selected Greek
texts. These patterns cannot be represented by a single Hebrew-versus-Greek
contrast or the uniform hundred-year adjustment seen in part of Genesis 5.

POB's numbers and direct Arpachshad-to-Shelah link accurately represent its
declared WLC source. They remain provisionally unchanged. An applied reader
note now discloses the additional generation and competing Arpachshad ages;
this comparison does not yet establish which complete textual history best
explains the others.
These are established published readings, not newly deciphered letters.

## Compared ages

Pairs are **age at the named son's birth / years afterward**. The two selected
Greek editions agree on these pairs, but do not represent all Greek witnesses.
“Absent” means no corresponding genealogical entry, not a missing or zero age.

| Person | Genesis 11 verses | WLC and POB | Tal–Florentin Samaritan | Swete and Rahlfs–Hanhart selected Greek | Samaritan explicit lifetime |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Shem | 10–11 | 100 / 500 | 100 / 500 | 100 / 500 | 600 |
| Arpachshad | 12–13 | 35 / 403 | 135 / 303 | 135 / 430 | 438 |
| Cainan | within Greek 13 | absent | absent | 130 / 330 | absent |
| Shelah | 14–15 | 30 / 403 | 130 / 303 | 130 / 330 | 433 |
| Eber | 16–17 | 34 / 430 | 134 / 270 | 134 / 370 | 404 |
| Peleg | 18–19 | 30 / 209 | 130 / 109 | 130 / 209 | 239 |
| Reu | 20–21 | 32 / 207 | 132 / 107 | 132 / 207 | 239 |
| Serug | 22–23 | 30 / 200 | 130 / 100 | 130 / 200 | 230 |
| Nahor | 24–25 | 29 / 119 | 79 / 69 | 79 / 129 | 148 |

The Samaritan edition explicitly prints all eight lifetime totals and death
formulas. WLC prints neither in these eight entries. The selected Greek texts
print death formulas but no separate lifetime totals. Consequently, WLC's
Eber lifetime of 464 and the selected Greek 504 are **computed sums**; the
Samaritan 404 is both explicitly printed and arithmetically consistent. Treating
all three as separately attested total clauses would manufacture evidence.

Five Samaritan pairs differ from WLC by +100/−100, conserving the sum:
Arpachshad, Shelah, Peleg, Reu and Serug. Nahor instead differs by +50/−50;
Eber differs by +100/−160, lowering the sum by 60. The Greek pairs have their
own pattern, including Arpachshad's 430 rather than WLC's 403. A uniform rule
cannot silently repair these actual readings into a preferred chronology.

If every link is consecutive, the begetting ages yield **220 years** from
Arpachshad's birth to Terah's in WLC, **870** in the Samaritan edition, and
**1,000** in each selected Greek edition. The Greek calculation includes
Cainan's 130 years. These relative sums exclude Shem's age of 100 and Terah's
grouped age of 70; they are not historical dates or a proof that the genealogy
must be used as a continuous clock. Verse 10's Hebrew two-year expression and
Greek second-year expression are retained as distinct wording rather than
silently equated with one exact elapsed duration.

## Terah and the limits of chronological harmonization

All four selected profiles give Terah 70 in 11:26, followed by Abram, Nahor
and Haran as a group. That wording does not establish simultaneous births or
Abram's individual birth at Terah's seventieth year. POB's existing note already
warns against treating the list as an exact birth order.

At 11:32, the Samaritan edition gives Terah's lifetime as **145**, whereas
WLC and both selected Greek editions give **205**. Neither side supplies a
separate number of years after the grouped births. Subtracting 70 from 145
gives 75, matching Abram's departure age in 12:4; this arithmetic alone does
not establish Abram's birth age, the sequence of every event, a scribal motive,
or which Terah reading is original. The family narrative beginning at 11:27
must not be treated as another ordinary two-line age entry.

Brooke–McLean also share editorial dependencies described in the prior report.

**Qumran.** No newly checked Qumran numeral or Cainan clause enters this
comparison. The large Genesis index could not be fetched through the web tool.
That access failure is not evidence that no relevant fragment exists, and
supplied parallel text must not be counted as surviving ink.

## Cainan across other works

The current WLC/POB texts at Genesis 10:24 and 1 Chronicles 1:18 directly
connect Arpachshad and Shelah. POB's declared SBLGNT text at Luke 3:36 includes
Cainan. This local cross-reference check establishes what POB currently prints;
it is not a fresh NT apparatus review or evidence that Luke independently
preserves an earlier Hebrew Genesis. No omission motive is inferred here.

A separate source reader directly checked [Jubilees 8:1–5](https://www.tau.ac.il/~hacohen/Jubil/Jubil%208.html)
in the TAU digital VanderKam edition: 8:1 identifies Kainan as Arpachshad's son,
and 8:5 makes him Shelah's father. The [provenance statement](https://www.tau.ac.il/~hacohen/Jubil/InformationVdK.html)
identifies the 1989 CSCO 510–511 Ethiopic edition and English translation,
without its apparatus and notes. This is a relevant literary parallel.
Whether this particular clause survives in an ancient Hebrew fragment, and
whether its wording is independent of Greek Genesis, remain unverified.
The composition date of Jubilees cannot substitute for those missing checks.

The [preservation and counting follow-up](#jubilees-counting-and-preservation)
below tests the literary argument and two Hebrew controls. It leaves the
chapter 8 clause's earliest physical attestation unresolved.

Jubilees therefore merits comparison as a witness to a related genealogical
tradition. That is a different question from recommending its inclusion in
a biblical canon; this passage alone cannot settle that community-dependent
judgment. Nor should its genealogy be inserted into Hebrew Genesis simply
because it agrees with a selected Greek text.

## POB implications and next discriminating evidence

The present comparison establishes consequential differences: an additional
generation, altered age relationships, explicit-versus-computed lifetimes and
a different Terah lifespan. POB's current Genesis 11 notes largely discuss
wording and name forms rather than these differences. The note now applied at
11:12 discloses the Cainan structure and Arpachshad ages. A wider chapter
summary remains useful for the other age relationships and Terah's lifespan.

The applied note at 11:12 reads:

> The Masoretic text, followed here, makes Arpachshad father Shelah at 35.
> The Greek text printed by Rahlfs–Hanhart instead gives Arpachshad 135 at
> Cainan's birth, then Cainan 130 at Shelah's birth (Greek verse 13). The
> Samaritan text printed by Tal and Florentin directly connects Arpachshad
> and Shelah but gives Arpachshad 135. The traditions also differ in the
> subsequent years and other ages in this genealogy.

This note describes the structural difference without treating a figure's
absence as a zero age. The application below preserves the three existing
note bodies and review history. No unverified reader link is inserted.

The strongest reopening argument is the agreement of the selected Samaritan
and Greek begetting ages, combined with the Cainan parallel in Jubilees. The
strongest restraint is that those agreements coexist with different subsequent
years, genealogy structure and transmission states. The current comparison
does not determine whether an age was expanded, reduced, harmonized or inherited
from a different Hebrew exemplar. Mechanical preference for older, shorter,
more coherent or more numerous witnesses cannot choose the original system.

Next, consult the modern critical apparatus at the Cainan clauses and the
Arpachshad/Nahor numerals, and establish the earliest physical attestation of
Jubilees 8:1–5. Those checks can discriminate histories more effectively than
another arithmetic tally. The original numerical record remains the historical
research receipt with its no-application flags; the separate application below
records the later reader change. Hebrew, marker-free main English and canonical
boundaries are unchanged. ImageGen cannot recover an unattested numeral or
generation by drawing plausible missing letters.

## Jubilees counting and preservation

The new [evidence record](../sources/textual_restoration/comparisons/genesis11_jubilees_followup.2026-10-16.v1.json)
compares opposing arguments for Jubilees 8:1–5 and checks what the relevant
Hebrew fragments actually attest in the consulted editions. **Genesis source
priority remains unresolved.** The added evidence makes an arithmetic argument
against Cainan less decisive; it does not establish an earlier Hebrew Genesis
with his name.

[Steinmann (2017), pp. 708–710](https://etsjets.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/02/files_JETS-PDFs_60_60-4_JETS_60_4-697-711_Steinmann.pdf),
uses Jubilees 2:23's 22 leaders with Jacob included to question Cainan's
originality. [Smith and Udd (2019), pp. 134–141](https://dbts.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/On-the-Authenticity-of-Kainan-Smith-and-Udd.pdf),
answer that Jacob follows 22 predecessors as the Sabbath follows 22 works.
The genealogical counts are:

| Genealogy | Including Jacob | Excluding Jacob |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Without the additional Cainan | 22 | 21 |
| With the additional Cainan | 23 | 22 |

The dispute therefore depends on how the endpoint and “leaders” are understood.
The Jacob/Sabbath parallel supports a plausible exclusive interpretation;
arithmetic alone does not prove that interpretation or date the genealogy.
Smith and Udd's p. 140 note 102 contains a numerical slip, assigning 22 without
Cainan to exclusive reckoning. The correct figure is 21; their main argument
uses the coherent exclusive count. This local error neither refutes nor
certifies the paper's wider source history.

**4Q216 supplies a narrower Hebrew control.** In the published
[transcription](https://lexicon.qumran-digital.org/transcriptions/4Q216/2026-05-21/index.html?v=2026-05-21),
displayed section 7, line 14 puts the first “22 leaders” expression wholly
inside supply brackets. Line 15 prints “from Adam until him” outside those
brackets. Root inspected lines 11–17 and the actual word/supply markup, not
just a reconstructed translation. The preserved endpoint wording is relevant
to interpretation, but does not name Cainan or supply a complete surviving
generation count. A second 22 in line 15 belongs to the works comparison;
its numeral has an editorial nun insertion, and its following context is partly
supplied. It cannot replace the supplied leadership count. An independent
reader checked both target Hebrew controls, preserving this distinction.
The [2018 VanderKam translation](https://palimpsest.stmarytx.edu/thanneken/2018/VanderKam(2018)Jubilees-Translation.html)
also distinguishes Scrolls evidence through underlining. Its unmarked chapter
8 passage cannot substitute for a complete fragment census.

**4Q180 requires its reconstruction history.** The current
[fragment 1, lines 4–5](https://lexicon.qumran-digital.org/transcriptions/4Q180/2026-10-06/index.html?v=2026-10-06)
does not print a complete Shem-to-Abraham ten-generation statement. Its number
expression carries doubt and an alternative; endpoints and the completed noun
are not supplied as secure surviving text in these rows. A fuller reconstructed
summary must not be counted as an explicit Hebrew omission of Cainan. This
does not disprove earlier reconstructions: the competing Allegro, Milik and
Dimant treatments and the photographs remain to be checked directly.

Smith and Udd also invoke Syriac wording and a proposed Hebrew confusion behind
“build” and “possess.” The initial counting packet consulted these through other
scholars; the [Syriac follow-up](#syriac-cainan-evidence-and-transmission) below
now checks the printed wording, without recovering its hypothetical Hebrew.
Narrative integration supports taking the Cainan passage seriously in Jubilees;
even original wording in Jubilees would still require a separate argument for
its relationship to Genesis. The known Latin palimpsest does not close the
chapter 8 gap: [Hanneken's manuscript account](https://jubilees.stmarytx.edu/Hanneken(2018)BookJubileesLatin.html)
places its extant content in chapters 13–49. Imaging erased writing on preserved
leaves and locating lost leaves are different evidence tasks. ImageGen adds
neither physical witness.

## Modern Greek apparatus and manuscript identity

Three bounded access routes did not yield Wevers's actual Genesis verse
apparatus. The [official publication list](https://septuaginta.uni-goettingen.de/publications/septuaginta/)
distinguishes the 1974 edition from its Notes and Text History; publisher access
failed, while the [Internet Archive copy](https://archive.org/details/genesis0001unse_l7x3)
is restricted. These access limits do not support any textual omission.

The accessible [author-prepared English introduction](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/gopher/text/religion/biblical/lxxvar/1Pentateuch/01Gen-Wevers-Intro.html),
prepared for the web in 2006, supplies a useful crosswalk in A.I, manuscript
**509**. Its parts are Oxford Bodleian Auct. T. inf. 2.1, Cambridge Add. 1879.7,
and the then-designated Leningrad Gr. 62. The majuscule portion corresponds to
Brooke–McLean **E**, the minuscule remainder to **a²**. The introduction places
Genesis 11 in the Oxford portion and reports collation from Tischendorf's 1857
edition. It does not establish the original/corrector reading at 11:13.

The next apparatus check must therefore track **509** and its local hand
notation rather than search only for E or count those edition labels as
different witnesses. Direct apparatus targets remain Genesis 11:12–13 and
11:24–25. The printed Syriac check is completed below; its ancestry remains open.
The applied reader note and the declared Hebrew remain appropriate pending
those discriminating checks. This comparison supplies no canon-expansion result.

## Syriac Cainan evidence and transmission

The [follow-up record](../sources/textual_restoration/comparisons/genesis11_syriac_followup.2026-10-16.v1.json)
pins the complete [Chabot scan](https://archive.org/details/chroniconanonymi01chab).
Its title page prints **1920**, despite the archive's 1916 label and our initial
private filename. Root visually checked printed p. 46 / PDF p. 307, including
the enlarged target; the volume's scanned body runs in descending page order.
Line 6 reads **ܕܢܒܢܐ ܠܗ ܐܬܪܐ ܘܡܕܝܢܬܐ**, “to build for himself a place and a
city,” in Qainan's narrative. An independent reader corrected root's tentative
abbreviation by retaining the intervening words and the conjunction. This is
confirmation of published Syriac, not newly deciphered manuscript ink.

Chabot's unnumbered *Monitum* / PDF p. 9 describes preserving some doubtful
readings from a transcript without always knowing their codex state. It does
not mark this building phrase doubtful. We inspected the printed edition, not
the manuscript or the whole critical apparatus. Syriac “build” does not uniquely
recover a Hebrew verb or prove the proposed Hebrew *b-n-h/q-n-h* confusion.

[Hilkens's thesis](https://biblio.ugent.be/publication/4381496), consulted in
bounded sections through indexed text, questions arguments for direct Hebrew
translation and even the existence of a complete Syriac Jubilees. Its account
dates the lost Fehim 4 copy only tentatively to the fourteenth century; that is
not the date of every tradition it contains. The local thesis download remained
incomplete and is not a verified PDF input. These cautions weaken the inference
of independent Hebrew ancestry; they do not disprove it.

[Coogan (2022), “Genealogy” and note 14](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/09518207211059474),
publishes a different Syriac list: BL Add MS 12154, ff. 180r–v, dated there to
the eighth century. Its post-Noah sequence names Arpachshad, his son Cainan,
then his son Shelah. Root read the published transcription/translation section,
not manuscript pixels or the whole article. This is a concrete earlier Syriac
parallel, not continuous Jubilees 8 prose. Coogan also reports VanderKam's case
for Greek mediation, against Tisserant's direct-Hebrew proposal.

**Decision:** retain the declared Hebrew, main English and applied reader note.
The evidence makes Cainan's wider transmission better documented, but supplies
neither an ancient Hebrew Jubilees 8 clause nor an independent Hebrew Genesis
reading. Complete Tisserant consultation, the modern Greek verse apparatus,
and a physical-witness census remain open. No new canonical recommendation
follows from this textual comparison.
