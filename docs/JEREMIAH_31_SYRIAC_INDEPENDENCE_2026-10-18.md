# Jeremiah 31 Syriac independence and early manuscript coverage

## Judgment

The Syriac negative response at Jeremiah 31:32 is securely present in the
previously checked critical text and its identified base manuscript. Its
independence from Greek is not established. Gillian Greenberg's original
argument specifically favors selective Greek influence here; it is more
relevant than simply noting that the Syriac translator generally used Hebrew.
The argument remains probabilistic, not proof of a different Hebrew spelling
or of Greek dependence at this word.

Retain the current Hebrew and the distinct Jeremiah and Hebrews main
translations provisionally. The [existing reader correction](JEREMIAH_31_READER_NOTE_CORRECTION_2026-10-18.md)
already discloses the meaningful alternative. This follow-up changes no source,
translation, note or reader code. No canon conclusion follows from this case.

## Greenberg addresses the actual pair

Greenberg's 1999 University of London PhD, *Translation Technique in the
Peshitta to Jeremiah*, is available through the [UCL institutional record](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1348821/)
and its [complete deposited thesis](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1348821/1/322699.pdf).
The argument is on printed pp. 275–277, PDF pp. 276–278. Her locator is
`31(LXX38):31(32)`; identification rests on the quoted Hebrew
אשר־המה הפרו את־בריתי ואנכי בעלתי בם and corresponding Syriac, not on that
locator alone. The thesis is not treated as the revised 2002 book or its later
electronic reissue.

She contrasts the favorable Syriac response in Jeremiah 3:14 with the negative
response in the new-covenant passage. For 3:14 she proposes influence from
Isaiah 62:4, where favorable disposition and marriage/mastery language occur
together. For 31:32 she regards a continuing positive relationship as better
suited to the forgiveness and new-covenant context. She considers the possible
relations between the clauses, but concludes that the Syriac's negative choice,
close to Greek disregard, suggests influence rather than independent convergence.
This is a local argument, not an assumption that every Syriac agreement depends
on Greek.

Her methodological discussion, printed pp. 225–229 / PDF pp. 226–230,
explicitly distinguishes agreement from dependence. Independent convergence,
different Hebrew exemplars and shared interpretation remain alternatives;
Greek misunderstanding or a distinctive blend can supply stronger evidence.
Those examples are her methodological comparisons, not new POB adjudications
of the other verses. Printed pp. 23–25 explain the practical selection of 7a1
and use of Walter's collations. The thesis, later critical edition and
facsimile must not become three independent ancient votes.

## What the argument establishes

The Hebrew controls in Isaiah 62:4–5 confirm the proposed relationship between
delight and marriage/mastery vocabulary. Isaiah 61:5–9 includes the covenant
context, and Jeremiah 31:33–34 promises renewed relationship and forgiveness.
These are relevant contextual checks, not evidence that a translator actually
borrowed from Isaiah or consulted Greek.

Our assessment differs from treating Greenberg's contextual preference as
decisive: a future promise of forgiveness does not exclude an earlier negative
response to a broken covenant. The target argument supplies no uniquely
discriminating shared error. The same-spelling negative Hebrew interpretation
discussed in the [lexical follow-up](JEREMIAH_31_LEXICAL_AND_VERSION_CONTROLS_2026-10-18.md)
also remains a competing explanation, with its independent-usage limitation
intact. This consultation therefore strengthens the case against counting Syriac
as automatic independent Hebrew support, but does not prove dependence or
justify replacing the Hebrew.

One small correction matters for precise reporting. On printed p. 276 / PDF
p. 277, the visible thesis glosses ܒܣܝܬ ܒܗܘܢ as “I despised you”. The suffix
ܒܗܘܢ refers to **them**, consistent with Hebrew בם and Greek αὐτῶν. This is a
slip in the English gloss, not an OCR-only error or an attested Syriac variant.
It does not by itself invalidate her influence argument.

## Other early copies are still uncollated at this word

The exact [British Library Add MS 17105 record](https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/040-002094904)
describes the manuscript as sixth/seventh century; the
[Add MS 17106 record](https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/040-002094905)
gives seventh century. Both inspected records have empty Digitised Content
fields. The date 599 seen in the collection search belongs to adjacent
Add MS 17102, not 17105.

Wright's [publisher-supplied catalogue preview](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781463207823_A38590289/preview-9781463207823_A38590289.pdf),
printed pp. 22–23 / PDF pp. 32–33, describes XXXVI, Add 17105 / 6h14, as
sixth-century Estrangela with later Greek vowels. XXXVII, Add 17106 / 7h8,
is sixth/seventh-century Estrangela with stained and torn final leaves. Its
listed missing portions include Jeremiah 31:6–21 and 31:37 onward. Thus 31:32
falls within nominal coverage; exact surviving letters do not follow. Preserve
the catalogue date differences rather than manufacture an exact year.

The [previously checked critical introduction](JEREMIAH_31_CRITICAL_SYRIAC_AND_MANUSCRIPT_2026-10-18.md)
reports 6h14 reinking at 31:3–30, not a verified target hand state, and damage
to 7h8 involving 31:31f. Neither catalogue coverage nor absence of an attached
electronic variant note establishes the wording or legibility of either copy
at the negative verb. Neither is counted as agreement.
