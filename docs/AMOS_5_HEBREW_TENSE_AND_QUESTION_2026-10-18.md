# Amos 5 carrying tense and the scope of the question

## Judgment

Amos 5:26 has at least three consequential constructions: a past assertion
contrasting with the question in verse 25, a continuation of that question,
or a future prediction linked to exile in verse 27. The inspected Hebrew and
original arguments do not uniquely determine one. Retain the source and
main English provisionally; this follow-up authorizes no canonical or reader
edit. A past interpretation does not by itself establish an assertion of
wilderness idolatry, and a future interpretation does not require newly
recovered consonants.

This extends the [Amos and Acts comparison](AMOS_5_ACTS_7_SOURCE_COMPARISON_2026-10-18.md),
not its manuscript census. Its contribution is a tested limitation on the
tense argument and recognition of the question-scope alternative. It supplies
no new manuscript letters, historical-priority verdict or canon proposal.

## What the Hebrew leaves open

The local WLC source has a question beginning הַזְּבָחִים in verse 25, followed
by הִגַּשְׁתֶּם and an explicit wilderness time setting. Verse 26 begins
וּנְשָׂאתֶם, ends its relative clause with אֲשֶׁר עֲשִׂיתֶם לָכֶם, and verse
27 begins וְהִגְלֵיתִי. Calling the middle verb a perfect with waw does not
alone choose its temporal relation or decide whether the question continues.
The lack of a repeated interrogative particle in verse 26 is not, by itself,
an affirmative assertion. No vowel or consonant change is proposed here.

| Construction | What it would mean | Decisive issue still open |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Past assertion | Idol carrying contrasts with the preceding sacrifice question | Whether the discourse supplies this adversative assertion |
| Past question | Verse 26 remains within, or continues, the preceding question | The question's scope, expected answer and any implied present accusation |
| Future prediction | Carrying belongs with the announced exile | Whether verse 27 rather than verse 25 supplies the temporal frame |

These are constructions, not complete competing translations. Deity names,
tent wording and the star/images arrangement require their own decisions.
The selected Greek past form recorded in the predecessor is evidence of an
ancient interpretation, not a second independent Hebrew manuscript or a
mechanical ruling on Hebrew syntax.

## The grammar expressly allows alternatives

Gesenius's *Hebrew Grammar*, second English edition revised by A. E. Cowley
(1910), §112x allows a loosely connected future use here and points to §112rr.
The latter includes the same verse among possible frequentatives and points
back to the future alternative. Citing x while omitting rr would overstate
the grammar's conclusion. The consulted [Palmer-hosted digital edition](https://tmcdaniel.palmerseminary.edu/GeseniusGrammar.pdf)
reflows the work: PDF pages 305–309 are not original printed-page locators.
The [edition transcription](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Gesenius%27_Hebrew_Grammar/112._The_Perfect_with_W%C4%81w_Consecutive)
also exposes the x and rr subdivision labels.

Source-only controls illustrate why context matters. First Samuel 1:3 has
וְעָלָה with an explicit annual past setting. Exodus 6:6–8 has a prospective
chain after divine self-identification; Genesis 26:22 is another of the
grammar's past-fact/prospective-continuation examples. They establish available
uses, not a distributional proof that Amos has one particular construction.

## Contrary readings in the original arguments

S. R. Driver, *The Books of Joel and Amos, with Introduction and Notes*
(Cambridge, stereotyped edition 1901), printed pp. 188–190 / PDF 187–189,
[argues for future carrying with exile](https://biblicalstudies.org.uk/pdf/e-books/driver_s-r/joel-and-amos_driver.pdf).
His note on printed p. 189 explicitly admits that the past is grammatically
possible. His preference depends on the passage's thought: verse 24 calls for
justice, verse 25 contrasts wilderness favor with ritual, and verses 26–27
announce exile with the cult objects. He notes that Hebrew verse 27 contains
no explicit therefore. He also acknowledges objections about conquerors
carrying gods and the apparent focus elsewhere on excessive Yahweh worship.
His historical accommodation of those objections is not grammatical proof.

C. F. Keil, *The Twelve Minor Prophets*, volume I, translated by James Martin
(Edinburgh: T. & T. Clark, 1878), printed pp. 289–291 and 296 / PDF 301–303
and 308, supplies a genuine contrary [past and adversative account](https://archive.org/download/thetwelveminorp01keiluoft/thetwelveminorp01keiluoft.pdf).
He understands verse 24 as divine punitive justice, verse 25 as a corporate
question about the wilderness generation, and verse 26 as contrasting
continued idol service. Verse 27 specifies the coming punishment. His
conqueror-versus-captive objection depends on who would carry cult objects;
it does not make the future construction morphologically impossible.
The English translation, not Keil's German original, was consulted.

J. Blair Wilgus, *Judgment on Israel: Amos 3–6 Read as a Unity* (PhD thesis,
University of Edinburgh, School of Divinity, 2012), printed pp. 157–162 /
PDF 158–163, argues for a [continuing rhetorical question](https://era.ed.ac.uk/bitstream/handle/1842/6428/Wilgus2012.pdf?sequence=2).
He ultimately favors a single question comparing sacrifice paired with other
worship to sacrifice paired with present injustice. The implied negative
answer and the analogy with oppression are his contextual reconstruction;
he acknowledges that oppression is not expressed in the immediate unit.
His references to Paul, Wolff, Jeremias, de Waard and Smalley, Garrett,
Joüon and IBHS are reported arguments, not originals consulted in this test.
His declaration that the adversative option has been abandoned is not
adopted as a settled fact: Keil supplies an inspectable example of it, though
not proof of its standing in current scholarship.

## Tests against the source context

Our assessment separates four inferences from those authors' conclusions.

1. Amos 5:7, 5:14–15 and 6:12 repeatedly associate justice and righteousness
   with human conduct. These give concrete same-book support for an ethical
   reading of 5:24, but do not alone settle 5:26's tense or question scope.
2. The relative past עֲשִׂיתֶם does not exclude future carrying of objects
   already made. Exodus 6:8 combines prospective bringing with a relative
   clause describing a prior oath. This counterexample defeats a categorical
   tense-exclusion argument, not Wilgus's entire contextual reading.
3. Jeremiah 48:7 and 49:3 picture deities going into exile with their cult
   personnel. Isaiah 46:1–2 pictures cult objects as burdens and in captivity.
   None of these passages by itself identifies the human carrier in Amos.
   They make deportation imagery relevant without proving Driver's scenario
   or Keil's exclusion of it.
4. Amos 8:14 condemns oaths associated with Samaria, Dan and Beersheba. Its
   precise cultic referents need argument; a blanket claim that Amos contains
   no other relevant cult accusation cannot eliminate an interpretation of
   5:26. Conversely, such an accusation would not prove a particular deity
   identification or the date of every layer in the book.

The continuing-question construction therefore deserves explicit comparison,
but neither it nor the future construction passes the threshold for replacing
the present English on these inputs alone. A negative answer to a compound
question also does not logically establish the negation of each component.
No harmonization to Acts, historical reconstruction of cult practice, or
preferred theology is allowed to decide a grammatical ambiguity silently.
