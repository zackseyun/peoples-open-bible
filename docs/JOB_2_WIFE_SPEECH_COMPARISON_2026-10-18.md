# Job 2 and the longer Greek speech of Job's wife

## Working judgment

The Greek scene materially changes the wife's voice, not merely its length.
Her account of bereavement, bodily suffering and service supplies a perspective
absent from the brief Hebrew speech. The closing Greek command is also less
explicit than POB's contextual “Curse God.” Preserve the two forms separately;
do not insert the Greek speech into a reconstructed Hebrew text without evidence.
Provisionally retain POB's Hebrew and main English. This comparison establishes
a significant literary alternative, not a newly discovered passage or a unique
optimal translation.

## The connected scene

Swete II printed 519–524 / PDF 537–542 was read visually as six complete pages,
including apparatus. The speech is at printed 523–524 / PDF 541–542. An initial
extraction-based locator, PDF 526, was rejected when its image proved to be
Canticles 2. The PDF is not shown to have mismatched OCR/images; the book had
been misidentified. The sigla key and the caution about Ephraemi were also read
at PDF 15–16 and 18. These are historical edition reports, not newly inspected
manuscript hands or a complete modern apparatus.

| Part | Retained Hebrew form | Consulted Greek form |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | A question about continued integrity | Elapsed time, then a question quoting Job's continuing hope for deliverance |
| Family loss | No extended maternal speech here | Sons and daughters, her birth pangs and labour, and a vanished family legacy |
| Present hardship | Job's sores and ashes in the preceding scene | His worms and nights outside, plus her wandering and service from house to house |
| Final proposal | ברך אלהים ומת | A command to say something to the Lord and die; no explicit Greek curse verb |
| Job's answer | Comparison with foolish women's speech, good/bad received from God, no sin with his lips | A look before speaking, the same basic rebuke and good/bad contrast, followed by the narrator's approval |

The Greek waiting words are quoted as Job's words inside her question; they
are not simply her own profession of hope. “Deliverance” does not by itself
establish a doctrine of resurrection. Nor does **λάτρις** alone establish wages
or a specific employment contract. The unqualified look at 2:10 does not say
that Job looks up to heaven. Those details must not enter an English rendering
as though they were explicit source wording.

Swete prints **πλανωμένη** in 9d and reports noun-form variants in the apparatus.
Do not use the rarer **πλανῆτις** as though it were every Greek witness's wording
when testing authorship by vocabulary. The apparatus's Ephraemi coverage starts
within 2:12, after this speech; C is not an additional witness for its presence
or absence here. Its damaged text and historical editor's uncertainty need their
own treatment. Edition/body/apparatus agreement is not a fresh manuscript vote.

## Blessing language needs context

The actual prologue controls distinguish lexical form from the interpretation
of an utterance. Hebrew **ברך** appears in the four disputed contexts below,
but also in genuine blessing at 1:10 and 1:21. It cannot mechanically mean
“curse” everywhere.

| Unit | Greek selected wording or action | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| 1:5 | **κακὰ ἐνενόησαν πρὸς θεόν** | The children's possible inward offence is expressed without a blessing verb |
| 1:11 and 2:5 | **εὐλογήσει** | The Adversary's direct speech retains a blessing verb in a negative context |
| 1:10 and 1:21 | Blessing of work; the Lord's name blessed | Positive uses remain part of the same prologue |
| 2:9 | **τι ῥῆμα εἰς Κύριον** within the final command | The Greek does not itself specify what she wants Job to say |

POB's “Curse God” with its literal-form note is a defensible contextual reading
of the retained Hebrew, not a literal rendering of the Greek and not proof that
no other construal is possible. The written Hebrew has not been replaced by
**קלל**. Greek ambiguity does not recover that alternative Hebrew spelling.
Current 2:9 metadata separately claims literal “Bless” is in the main text,
contrary to the actual English. That inconsistency is recorded, not repaired
or treated as reliable historical approval in this research-only comparison.

## Published arguments and their limits

[Cook 2010](https://www.bsw.org/biblica/vol-91-2010/are-the-additions-in-lxx-job-2-9a-e-to-be-deemed-as-the-old-greek-text/444/),
pages 275–284, was read through all ten public article pages, including notes.
He argues that integration into the dialogue and shared vocabulary favour the
original translator, while allowing a later writer; he reads the wife as
challenging Job's fidelity. [Mangum 2020](https://doi.org/10.4102/hts.v76i4.6140)
was read in full, with PDF pages 4–6 inspected visually. He instead stresses
her shared losses and the less explicit command. His account builds on Cook
and Kutz, not independent manuscript evidence. Positions attributed to Heater
and Fernández Marcos remain indirect; their actual target studies were not read.

An integrated expansion can be the work of a translator or of a capable later
writer familiar with the book. Lexical overlap alone cannot identify its author.
The two modern interpretations also show why the Greek does not force a single
moral portrait of the wife. The additional speech makes her suffering explicit;
whether that primarily elicits sympathy or strengthens the challenge to Job is
a further interpretive judgment. Neither judgment establishes Hebrew priority.

The pinned biblical QDR data contain Job-labelled references for 2Q15,
4Q99, 4Q100 and 4Q101, but no Job 1–2 reference hits in those records.
This is a bounded index result, not proof that no ancient Hebrew or Aramaic
evidence exists. No Qumran ink or supplied reconstruction supports the longer
speech in this comparison. Later Targum material must not be relabelled as a
Qumran Aramaic witness.
