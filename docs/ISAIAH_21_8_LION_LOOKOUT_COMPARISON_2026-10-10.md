# Isaiah 21 8 lion and lookout source comparison

At baseline `61c8af3011b9501ab05ff6a0ffa829e980ffd6f7`, POB's “A lion!”
conceals a consequential published Hebrew alternative: “the one who sees,”
which can give “Then the lookout cried.” This is an existing variant, not a
new decipherment. **Retain the Masoretic source provisionally and hold historical
priority.** A separate reader disclosure and anchor repair is now applied; neither
better contextual fit nor manuscript age alone establishes the earliest reading.

## Competing explanations and English consequence

The lookout reading fits the watcher appointed in 21:6, his seeing/listening in
21:7 and the report in 21:9. It changes the apparent speaker identification:
not a cry naming a lion, but the lookout's cry. Its main objection is that such
an intelligible reading could be a contextual clarification of a difficult
inherited lion expression. Contextual ease does not independently establish
priority. Conversely, lion could be a corruption of the lookout expression;
these consonantal strings are not merely two vowel pointings or a demonstrated
single-letter copying error. No precise mechanism is established in this pass.

The strongest retained-reading explanation treats the lookout as crying
*like* a lion. [Ibn Ezra's Hebrew commentary](https://www.sefaria.org/Ibn_Ezra_on_Isaiah.21.8),
read through [Sefaria's text API](https://www.sefaria.org/api/texts/Ibn_Ezra_on_Isaiah.21.8?context=0),
explicitly supplies a comparative kaf and explains the loud cry. This is a
historical interpretation, not an additional ancient manuscript with that
prefix. The displayed Friedlander English says to supply bet instead; do not
silently substitute its wording for the Hebrew explanation. POB's current
main “A lion!” does not actually express the comparison its note and lexical
rationale claim. Latin retains a lion subject while Greek introduces Uriah;
neither selected version is a simple confirmation of the 1QIsaᵃ wording.

The early direct-Hebrew alternative deserves serious consideration under the
method's modest chronological preference, but local transmission remains
unresolved. Removing that preference does not alter this pass's hold. Retention
is an operational base choice, not a finding that the Masoretic wording wins.
Before changing the source/main English, inspect target modern critical
apparatus/commentary and test local 1QIsaᵃ clarification behavior and the Greek
name's explanation. Reopen for that discriminating evidence, not another
same-evidence preference vote. “My lord” and its existing divine-address
rationale are outside this comparison and receive no new certification.
