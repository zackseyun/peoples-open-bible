In the bow-down colon, Swete's B text has sons of God without all; its apparatus
adds all for A and reports all angels for F. The later strengthening colon has
all angels of God in B. The apparatus also reports omission of Lord for F in
the closing clause. These entries demonstrate local Greek diversity; silence
in this selective apparatus cannot establish universal agreement or absence.
No current Göttingen apparatus or Greek manuscript photograph was inspected.

Current POB Hebrews 1:6 has all angels of God in its SBLGNT quotation. It is not
the exact bow-down wording of Swete's B text, although a corresponding local
Greek reading is reported for F. This does not settle the quotation's precise
source, the direction of influence or an exact Hebrew Vorlage. Hebrews source,
English and notes remain untouched. NT theological reception does not select

## Competing transmission accounts

[Emanuel Tov's own 2023 article](https://www.thetorah.com/article/acclaim-o-nations-deuteronomy-32-43)
and all its notes were read. It favors the Qumran opening, links heavens to
the poem's beginning, treats the Greek paired calls as duplication, and proposes
that discomfort with divine beings led to revision in MT. This is a scholarly
transmission hypothesis, not observed scribal intent. Its displayed Hebrew
Greek reconstruction is not an attested additional Hebrew manuscript.

[Patrick W. Skehan, BASOR 136, December 1954, pp. 12–15](https://ru.scribd.com/document/408700715/A-fragment-of-the-song-of-Moses)
was read in its publicly exposed original article text, not the uploader's
automated summary. Root and the source assessor read the complete article,
including final-page note 4. Skehan supports heavens and sons but considers
the repayment wording adapted from verse 41 and the worship wording possibly
borrowed from Psalm 97:7. He regards Qumran ויכפר as later verbal syntax than
MT וכפר. His four-colon reconstruction is unattested and is not unchanged MT.
His final-column stitching/margin argument informs the excerpt question, but
his preliminary classification predates DJD and cannot replace that gate.

Root checked the actual local Hebrew at Deuteronomy 32:41 and Psalm 97:7.
Their wording supplies concrete repetition/borrowing models, not proof of
direction. Qumran's sons fits the poem's family vocabulary, and a heavens/
homage pair supplies poetic closure. Conversely, an excerpted liturgical ending
could acquire that pair; regular parallelism may be a revision rather than an
original feature. No single copying accident explains every difference. The
Greek's extra pair makes expansion/conflation plausible without making its
exact lost Hebrew recoverable. These accounts warrant a whole-form hold, not
automatic selection of the longest or most theologically attractive form.

The physical Qumran evidence gives earlier attestation than medieval controls,
but not an object-specific dating determination in this pass. Chronology modestly
strengthens the six-colon hypothesis; without it, the overlapping Hebrew/Greek
features remain significant but priority is weaker. It cannot override the
concrete morphological and adaptation objections or settle every unit together.

The source assessor additionally consulted Dvoracek's 2016 thesis pp. 22–24,
a survey favoring six-colon ancestry; root does not rely on that survey for the
decision or diplomatic wording. Park's September 30, 2026 article, DOI
10.24333/jkots.2026.32.3.46, was located only through metadata/abstract. Its
full argument is unconsulted, as are Belenkaja's 2019 chapter, van der Kooij's
1994 chapter and DJD XIV pp. 137–142. Bibliographic leads are not full readings.
