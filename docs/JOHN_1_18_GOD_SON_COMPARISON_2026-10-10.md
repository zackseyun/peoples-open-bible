Retain the current SBLGNT source and main English provisionally; earliest
wording and whole-verse English optimality remain unresolved. Directly consulted
published Greek transcriptions support God, while an institutional majuscule
apparatus also records Son. Article presence, neighboring corrections and
English interpretation are separate questions. This is comparison of known
evidence, not newly recovered manuscript letters or a canon recommendation.

## Competing transmission and English interpretations

[Brian Wright's original John 1:18 argument](https://bible.org/article/jesus-%CE%B8%CE%B5%CF%8C%CF%82-god-textual-examination)
favors God and proposes article addition followed by substitution of Son.
He addresses familiar Johannine Son language, the unusual God phrase and
patristic ambiguity. His opponent citations and version lists are mediated
reports, not newly collated manuscripts or direct consultation of every cited
author. Root reads the complete target section and relevant footnotes.

The strongest counterhistory is an early branch changing Son into God, whether
through sacred-name confusion or deliberate Christological emphasis. Related
God witnesses could inherit that change. Wright's pre-fourth-century dating
argument would weaken a specifically fourth-century origin if accepted, but
does not rule out an earlier alteration. The papyrus dates are not independently
adjudicated here. Conversely,
familiar Son language can explain harmonization away from an unusual God phrase.
These are competing histories, not observed motives. The Sinaiticus article
addition alone does not prove a universal three-stage sequence.

The [NET translators' own note](https://classic.net.bible.org/verse.php?book=Joh&chapter=1&verse=18)
prefers God but interprets the adjective substantivally with God in apposition.
That yields a unique-one description followed by a God description; POB instead
uses one adjective-noun phrase. Thus changing English need not require changing
the Greek noun. Article absence alone does not compel an indefinite English
article or settle Christology. The current POB rendering and the appositional
alternative need a separate grammatical comparison before replacement.

## Outcome and next evidence

The existing Greek and English remain unchanged. The published God
attestations support retaining that live option; Son's actual Greek attestation
and plausible opposing history prevent declaring God demonstrably earliest.
No modern translation majority, theological preference or same-model vote
settles the question. One independent assessment supports qualified retention;
its subsequent check of the genuinely new institutional units independently
matches both hashes and verifies the positive Son attestations and separate
Sinaiticus corrections. Source priority remains held. This is not a blinded
fresh transcription or full English candidate review.

The current notes have separate anchor defects. Note a explains the second God
phrase but follows the initial God; note c explains has made him known but
follows Father. Note b concerns the bosom phrase. A future exact candidate can
repair anchors and clarify source versus interpretation while preserving the

John export. No candidate or canonical change is applied in this report.

## Retained Greek English comparison

| Phrase | Interpretation and possible gain | Material cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A: the one and only, himself God | Unique person followed by an appositional God description; avoids the immediate English sense of sole God | Commits to apposition and introduces potentially emphatic himself without a separate Greek equivalent |
| B: the one and only God | Compact adjective–noun description, currently used by POB | English can suggest sole God and obscure the revealer's relationship to the Father |

[Daniel Wallace's original grammatical argument](https://bible.org/article/text-and-grammar-john-118)
defends apposition and emphasizes the substantival use of monogenes in 1:14.
Root reads the full argument and footnotes; its Ehrman quotations remain mediated.
Our inference is that defeating an absolute grammatical prohibition would not,
by itself, establish apposition as the best reading of this particular phrase.

Direct examination of the retained SBLGNT controls adds an important limitation.
Romans 1:30 and 1 Timothy 1:9 are lists rather than one adjective–noun phrase.
Galatians 3:9 permits a faithful/believing Abraham interpretation as well as a
substantival analysis; its article and proper name also differ from John 1:18.
Second Peter 2:5 places an ordinal with Noah, not monogenes with God. These
examples show why matching inflection need not decide every construction;
they do not compel the proposed John apposition. John 1:14 has no immediately
following concord noun, and 1:1 has an explicit copula. Their literary relevance
therefore does not make either an exact syntactic parallel.

The fresh assessor consults Smyth's rules for
[adjective agreement and substantival use, 1020–1022](https://grammars.alpheios.net/smyth/xhtml/body.1_div1.4_div2.14.html)
and [apposition, 976 and 986–988](https://grammars.alpheios.net/smyth/xhtml/body.1_div1.4_div2.10.html).
Root subsequently reads the same target sections. These general, predominantly
classical rules establish available constructions, not verse-specific priority.
The assessor recommends HOLD: A's contextual clarity is plausible, but B's
compactness is not proof that its English uniqueness scope is fully faithful.
Their shared contextual object him in made him known does not distinguish them.

Candidate ownership is withheld from the assessor, who receives A before B,
reversing root's initial order. The assessor reports familiar phraseology but
does not confidently recognize the exact origins. This is limited identity
blinding, not full knowledge blinding, random sampling, two-family validation
or specialist certification. Root's analysis is recorded before the assessor's
result; agreement is not an additional manuscript witness.
