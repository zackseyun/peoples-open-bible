# Psalm 73 10 written and read Hebrew comparison

The recorded Masoretic reading supports a provisional change from “he brings his people back” to “his people return.” This changes agency, not merely English style. The written causative remains a substantial alternative; the earliest wording is unresolved. The difficult water clause and the referents of “his” and “here” remain unchanged.

## Hebrew evidence and context

Actual WLC/OSHB XML records unpointed ketiv ישיב, tagged Hiphil imperfect 3ms, followed by pointed qere יָשׁ֣וּב, Qal imperfect 3ms. The [comparison record](../sources/textual_restoration/comparisons/psalm73_10_written_read.2026-10-10.v1.json) pins the XML, word IDs, morphology and baseline. The independently consulted [UXLC archive](https://www.tanach.us/Books/Tanach.xml.zip) records the same written/read distinction. These are related digital controls of the Leningrad tradition, not two independent manuscripts. Expanding the unpointed ketiv to יָשִׁיב explains its grammar; it does not recover newly observed vowel marks.

With the qere, the singular collective “his people” naturally supplies the subject. English plural “return” does not change Hebrew singular morphology. The written alternative instead permits a causative subject bringing the people back. Its support is real, but its unnamed singular agent has no clear local antecedent. The existing footnote presents “return” as an alternative without clearly identifying the edition’s different reading form.

Psalm 73 moves from the prosperity and speech of the wicked in verses 3–12 through the speaker’s crisis to the sanctuary perspective and eventual confidence. Neither that trajectory nor the nearby plural “they say” establishes whose people these are or where “here” points. Selecting the qere does not settle those questions.

The strongest objection is that the reading tradition may have regularized a difficult earlier written form. Better syntax is not proof of historical priority. No discriminating chronological Hebrew contrast was acquired; removing chronological preference leaves the provisional choice unchanged. [Published GKC §72](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Gesenius%27_Hebrew_Grammar/72._Verbs_%D7%A2%D7%B4%D7%95%D6%BC) explains the hollow-verb distinction. A lexicographer’s proposed “satisfies them with bread” is conjecture, not manuscript evidence established here.

The versions broadly support people as the returning subject, but do not establish exact Hebrew back-translation. Their possessive and second-clause differences must be considered separately. No hybrid “my people” or “full days” is inserted into the selected Hebrew verse. The Hebrew-based Latin’s passive or middle expression does not uniquely prove Hebrew pointing.

Complete publisher chapters and edition footers were read; the mouths/heaven, disputed people, divine-knowledge question and wealthy-wicked sequence aligns Greek/Latin Psalm 72 with Hebrew Psalm 73. Swete II’s complete printed pages 307–308 and its own preface/sigla were visually consulted by the Greek/Latin agent. Its selective apparatus reports differences in the introductory phrase, article before “days” and preposition before “them,” not a decisive Hebrew verb. Alexandrinus has a reported lacuna including this verse, so it supplies no target support here. The scan’s imprint year was not verified.

CAL’s [metadata](https://cal.huc.edu/get_file_info.php?coord=62027) identifies a selected critical compilation, not a fresh reading of manuscript 7a1 or unanimous Syriac transmission. The actual chapter parameter is 073; the earlier 73 response was an access error, not missing source text.

A local QDR screen finds no indexed Psalm 73:9–12 witness. Its positive control correctly returns Psalm 22:17 witnesses. This means no local indexed target coverage, not an attested omission or a census of every discovered Dead Sea Scroll. A modern Göttingen apparatus and new physical Hebrew witness have not been acquired, so no comprehensive apparatus or original-text verdict follows.
