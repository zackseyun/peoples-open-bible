# Ezekiel 28:14–16: the king and the cherub

## Outcome

**Retain the pointed WLC form provisionally; hold historical priority.** The
selected Greek distinguishes the king from the cherub, whereas the pointed
Hebrew identifies/addresses him as a cherub. This is a consequential, known
textual/interpretive difference, not newly recovered Hebrew. The source-only
comparison made no canonical change. The subsequent
[reader application](#separate-reader-disclosure-application) applies qualified
notes and repaired anchors while preserving source and marker-free English;
its records remain drafts without publication approval.

## Hebrew checks that discriminate

The actual short pointed **אַתְּ** can address a male: WLC Numbers 11:15 and
Deuteronomy 5:27 provide controls without a ketiv/qere substitution. The latter
has both long and short forms addressing Moses. OSHB tags the Numbers form
masculine and the Deuteronomy form feminine despite masculine context.
Therefore the Ezekiel morphology tag alone cannot diagnose corruption.
This establishes grammatical possibility, not historical priority.

The actual WLC controls at 1 Samuel 24:19, Job 1:10, Psalm 6:4,
Ecclesiastes 7:22 and Nehemiah 9:6 have short consonantal ketiv and expanded
masculine qere. Do not conflate these with the two pointed short-form controls.
The [BDB 859](https://biblehub.com/bdb/859.htm) Deuteronomy 5:24 locator
aligns by content with WLC 5:27; WLC 5:24 lacks this target. WLC itself marks
1 Samuel 24:19 and Psalm 6:4 as corresponding to KJV 24:18 and 6:3.

Reading את as the preposition “with” is a serious alternative consonantal
analysis, but not the fixed WLC vocalization. Likewise, making the cherub
the agent in 16 requires an explicit alternative verb form/analysis. It
cannot be advertised as a more fluent translation of unchanged pointed
Hebrew. The selected Greek also has “wounded” and “led/brought,” not simply
the Hebrew first-person destruction with its subject swapped.

[BDB 4473](https://biblehub.com/bdb/4473.htm) marks *mimshach* as disputed,
discussing anointed and expansion/spreading analyses. The expansion proposal
fits a covering cherub and the inspected Latin rendering; the anointing
proposal has the familiar root connection. Neither alone establishes the
earliest wording. Cornill's reported deletion/rearrangement is a conjecture,
not an acquired Hebrew manuscript. No fresh HALOT consultation occurred.

## Competing transmission explanations

The strongest case for the Greek-side analysis is its connected distinction
at both 14 and 16, not a feminine-looking pronoun in isolation. Greek could
preserve an earlier source interpretation or form subsequently made more
explicit in Hebrew. Conversely, a translator could differentiate the king
and cherub while interpreting difficult consonants and compressing modifiers.
Neither mechanism is demonstrated merely by the selected text.

The Hebrew-side case is that a coherent, unusual pronoun plus cherub
description and vocative can explain why later readers might regularize it.
Its strongest objection is that coherence and difficulty do not exclude
secondary elaboration; the connected Greek contrast must not be dismissed
just because WLC is POB's base. Neither “harder” nor “shorter” wins by rule.

Lydia Lee's [2021 article](https://doi.org/10.3390/rel12020091), relevant
published pages 6 and 11–13, offers a counterargument to Greek priority while
proposing some secondary Hebrew additions. Its tables contain reconstructed
Hebrew, not newly preserved Hebrew. Lee reports a mixed Peshitta: accompaniment
in 14 but divine destruction/vocative in 16, and discusses fragmentary later
Greek recensions. Those reports caution against treating versions as uniform
votes. This pass did not independently acquire the Syriac or verify those
recensional readings. Lee's translator-intention explanation remains an
argument, not an observed ancient motive. The cited originals were not read.

The modest chronological preference gives early Greek evidence a reason to
be investigated, but cannot make a source spelling uniquely recoverable
through translation. Modern edition dates are not ancient copy dates; no
individual Greek manuscript/hand chronology was independently verified here.
Removing that preference leaves the same outcome: retain the declared base
provisionally and hold original priority. Secure selected-edition attestation
is not secure reconstruction of an autograph.

## English consequence and stopping rule

POB currently presents an anointed guardian cherub in 14 and divine destruction
of that cherub in 16. A reader disclosure should eventually describe the
connected Greek alternative without claiming it is merely another rendering
of unchanged pointed WLC. The rare adjective also deserves qualified lexical
disclosure. Neither form identifies Satan or Adam by name; source selection
does not resolve those later interpretations or change the canon.

The full target records also have existing note-anchor/record inconsistencies:
14's short-pronoun note is anchored to the mountain phrase; 16's profaning
note is anchored to the cherub. These are identified for a separate exact-record
application, not silently repaired here. Historic lexical labels and model
agreement are not current adjudication approval. This pass does not certify
the rest of chapter 28 or call all existing notes satisfactory.

Reopen the **source-priority** hold only with discriminating new evidence:
verified Greek apparatus units/hand assignments and dependencies, an
independently inspected Syriac control, a relevant direct Hebrew witness, or
a new local translation-behavior/transmission argument. Do not repeat the
same comparison or commission judges until they agree. A separate bounded
reader-note application can proceed without claiming original priority.
