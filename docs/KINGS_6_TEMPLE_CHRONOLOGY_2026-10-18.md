# Temple chronology in Hebrew and Greek Kings

## Assessment

The temple account has more than a single 480 versus 440 variant. Its Greek
transmission also varies the placement of foundation and completion dates,
the explicit seven-year duration, and whether the palace clause expresses
thirteen years of work or a thirteenth-year date. These features do not form
one uniform package. The connected evidence does not establish a coordinated
forty-year chronological revision or a unique copying error.

Retain POB's Hebrew and English provisionally. There is a reasonable working
preference for 440 in the earliest recoverable Greek at 6:1, distinct from
selection of the earliest Hebrew number. Neither a date for the historical
Exodus nor a new ancient reading is established here. This comparison adds
published evidence and countertests to POB, not a discovery new to scholarship.

## The actual apparatus and connected dates

Brooke, McLean and Thackeray, *The Old Testament in Greek* II.2 (1930),
printed 223–224, 232 and 242 / PDF 35–36, 44 and 54, supplies the following
controls. Its body follows Vaticanus, not a reconstructed original Greek.
Its own preface and inherited Samuel/Genesis key distinguish selected cursives,
corrections, critical signs and Holmes–Parsons reports. In particular,
b combines 19 and 108; i is 56, n is 119, o is 82, x is 247,
c₂ is 127 and e₂ is 93. Editions and quotations do not add manuscript votes.

| Unit in Hebrew numbering | Hebrew source | Greek body and consequential apparatus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 6:1 | 480th year from the Exodus; Solomon's fourth year, second month | Body has 440th. Z b i c₂ e₂ substitute eighty for forty. x reverses the components but still has 440. B's correction report concerns spelling, not a change to eighty. |
| 6:37–38 | Fourth-year foundation, eleventh-year completion, explicit seven years | Body moves the two dates before the dimensions, at local 6:4–5. Its completion clause lacks explicit seven years; i n o add a seven-year expression. A separate apparatus block places the dates, including seven years, before local 5:17 in a partly overlapping witness set. These are not simply lost dates. |
| 7:1 | Solomon's own house takes thirteen years | Body moves this after the temple furnishings, at local 7:38. b o c₂ e₂ have a thirteenth-year construction instead of a thirteen-year duration. Z has partly unreadable regnal-year wording. i j u retain a three-and-ten-years duration form. |
| 9:10 | Twenty years building the two houses | Body also has twenty years for both houses. No different numeral is explicitly reported in this inspected unit; silence is not universal agreement. |
| 2 Chronicles 3:2 and 8:1 | Fourth-year start; twenty years for both houses | Hebrew parallel controls only; the Greek Chronicles apparatus was not consulted here. |

The 480 witnesses cross the palace distinction: i retains duration wording,
whereas b c₂ e₂ use an ordinal-year construction. Conversely, o has the
ordinal palace construction without being named for 480. Thus neither
480 nor 440 identifies a single chronology throughout these units. The
body's fourth/eleventh dates and thirteen/twenty durations also provide no
local forty-year offset. The arithmetic seven plus thirteen equals twenty
is compatible with one account, not a proof of sequence in every witness.

The body also gives forty cubits for the temple's length, against Hebrew
sixty and reported Greek alternatives. This architectural forty is relevant
to a proposed copying mechanism, but is not forty elapsed years.

## Two original arguments and their limits

Henry B. Smith Jr.'s [original article](https://biblearchaeology.org/images/Smith-Nov-2025-newsletter-article.pdf),
*NEAS Bulletin* 69 (2024), 41–64, was posted on the author's institutional
site in October 2025. Its actual Table 2 reports twenty-four Greek copies
with 440 and seven with 480, not the counts in a derivative web summary.
Those are his collated reports, not thirty-one independently reread objects
in this comparison. His Rahlfs/Göttingen column contains catalogue IDs,
not results from a released critical Kings edition.

Smith explicitly regards 440 as the likely Old Greek number while advocating
480 as the original Hebrew number. He acknowledges that the textual case
alone is not decisive. His proposed pre-Christian local 480 depends on
reconstructing the ancestry of later Antiochene witnesses. Early ancestry of
a textual group does not by itself date this particular reading. Preserving
non-Masoretic numbers elsewhere does not rule out correction toward Hebrew
at 6:1. His own verb-form discussion records exceptions requiring further
verification. M's transmitted Aquila/Symmachus margin is not a surviving
second-century autograph or two additional physical codices.

His chronological conclusion depends on a 967 BCE foundation date, 49-year
jubilee cycles and historically accurate sixteenth/seventeenth jubilee counts
in later rabbinic traditions. A secure late-monarchy date cannot alone prove
how many earlier cycles elapsed. The related jubilee traditions and their
calculations are not wholly independent confirmations. This comparison does
not independently certify or refute the underlying archaeological studies.

The appendix proposes Hebrew harmonization, Hebrew conflation or Greek
contamination from the temple's forty-cubit length. Its printed Greek example
uses ὀκτακοστῷ for eighty, whereas the consulted numeral apparatus reports
ὀγδοηκοστῷ. The proposed shared-letter illustration therefore is not a literal
demonstration using that apparatus form. Contamination remains a possible
general explanation, not an observed error or a uniquely established cause.

Martin Meiser's [publisher-authorized preview](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9783161617584_A43750745/preview-9783161617584_A43750745.pdf)
of *The Septuagint and Its Reception* (2022), printed 41–43, includes the
actual discussion on 42 and its notes. It describes 480 as twelve times forty
and suggests 440 could harmonize eleven priestly generations in 1 Chronicles
5:29–34. The directly checked Hebrew genealogy supplies relationships, not
forty-year terms of priestly service. Selecting endpoints and assigning a
standard duration remain explanatory premises; factorization alone does not
date a revision. The whole book and the cited commentaries were not read.

Both arguments are useful alternatives. Neither the symbolic account nor
the jubilee account uniquely establishes which Hebrew reading preceded the
other. Preference for the recoverable Greek must remain separate from
preference for a hypothesized Hebrew exemplar and from historical accuracy.
