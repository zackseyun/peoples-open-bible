# Solomon temple poem Greek witnesses and a competing reconstruction

## Assessment

The newly consulted historical Kings apparatus closes part of the earlier
temple-poem access gap. It explicitly reports both the sun-verb alternative
and Greek **μοι**, so the latter is no longer only a proposed Greek equivalent
of Latin *mihi* in this investigation. It also reveals materially mixed wording
within the often grouped Greek witnesses. These are known published readings,
not newly discovered letters.

There is a reasonable **working preference for retaining ἐγνώρισεν at the
Greek sun-verb unit**, rather than replacing it with ἐστήσεν merely because
the latter is clearer. This does not recover the sun clause's Hebrew or select
the earliest complete poem. Hebrew priority remains **HOLD**. Current POB
Hebrew, English and reader notes are unchanged.

## The broader historical apparatus

Brooke, McLean and Thackeray, *The Old Testament in Greek*, II.2 (1930),
printed **235/PDF 47** and **239/PDF 51**, supplies the two locations.
It was acquired from the
[Palmer Seminary collection](https://tmcdaniel.palmerseminary.edu/Brooke%26McLean/LXX_Brooke%26McLean_2-2.pdf)
during the [comparative-formula study](KINGS_COMPARATIVE_FORMULAS_2026-10-17.md).
The modern Antiochene edition remains unacquired. This is a selected historical
apparatus, not a complete 2026 witness census or a reconstructed earliest text.
Its B-based body reuses Swete; agreement between these editions is not another
ancient witness.

Kings' own preface adopts Samuel's key and the inherited Genesis conventions.
The relevant definitions were read directly: **b = 19 + 108**, o = 82,
c₂ = 127, e₂ = 93, i = 56, g = 158 and x = 247. A is Alexandrinus;
M is Coislinianus, historically Paris Coislin Gr. 1. Kings' own Z description
identifies its Kings fragments, not another occurrence of an unrelated Z.
These historical shelfmarks are not a new audit of present custody or dates.

The key distinguishes the original-hand superscript `*` from the printed
Hexaplaric asterisk sign. For h, superscript b denotes a later correction,
not another manuscript. `vid` is a qualification; angle brackets identify
Holmes-and-Parsons-authority information. `rell` and `omn` concern the
selected cursives, not every surviving manuscript. The complete variant list
governs an individual witness's wording.

The explicit c₂ reports combine an early short insertion, later long material
with critical marking, a different sun verb, subject omission, a dative building
expression and altered surrounding wording. Calling all this one undifferentiated
“Lucianic poem” hides evidence relevant to transmission. Conversely, the signs
alone do not prove who made a revision, its motive or its date.

## Testing the sun verb rather than counting witnesses

F. C. Burkitt's actual 1909 article, printed 439–446, is now consulted through a
[public historical scan](https://biblicalstudies.org.uk/pdf/jts/010_439.pdf).
He argues at 440–441 that deriving ἐγνώρισεν from a misread **הבין** is a weak
link in a proposed Hebrew palaeographic explanation. His alternative is an
ordinary correspondence with **הודיע**, followed by Greek contextual smoothing
to “set.” That mechanism does not require a second Hebrew source at this unit.
It remains an explanation, not an observed act of correction.

A positive local control is now checked directly: Hebrew **1 Kings 1:27** has
הודעת, WLC word ID `11Azu`, annotated as ידע Hiphil. The BM selected Greek has
**ἐγνώρισας**, printed 203–204/PDF 15–16. No replacement of that verb is
explicitly reported in the inspected unit; surrounding variants do not certify
whole-verse agreement. The written/qere distinction there affects “servant(s),”
not this verb. One positive correspondence supports the lexical countermodel;
it does not prove an always/never rule across the Septuagint.

The working Greek preference rests on that positive analogy and the intelligible
direction from an unusual expression to an easier contextual one, not on a
rule that difficulty guarantees age. B and A's earlier physical attestation
within the initially examined pair supplies only the modest chronological tilt
already documented there. Removing that tilt leaves the same tentative
preference. The strongest objection remains that “set” has explicit Greek
support and a corresponding Latin report and could preserve an early source
reading. The present comparison does not date its origin or eliminate that
possibility. Translation age is not the date of a particular marginal reading.

Burkitt's own further proposals, an imperative “shine forth” and a New-Moon
interpretation, are conjectural. His broad restriction to two Hebrew streams
at 443–446 is not established by this one local mechanism and is not adopted.
The article adds no new target Latin transcription; its Vienna references
control citation formulas at other passages.
Even his 441 footnote allows Book of Song, rather than Jashar, as the original
title. Neither identification supplies the missing work.

## The newly consulted countermodel

Szabolcs-Ferencz Kató's 2019
[author copy](https://www.proteo.cj.edu.ro/sites/default/files/documents/publications/2019/1560928057.pdf),
*ZAW* 131, 220–234, also treats “set” as possible Greek clarification. He
proposes a missing Hebrew **לשבת**, making the reconstructed sun clause parallel
to Yahweh's dwelling statement. He then considers distinct divine figures,
not Pakkala's identification of sun and Yahweh. He favors parallel use of a
faulty source while acknowledging that the reconstruction fits another
transmission model too. These are hypotheses, not newly attested Hebrew.

The proposed omission has no surviving target letters or independent layout
measurement here. The actual Greek has accusative ἥλιον; its conversion to
a speaking Hebrew subject requires retroversion and clause division. The
parallelism table at 231 itself includes incomplete and differing structures,
so symmetry does not compel the supplied verb. The checked Hebrew poem in
2 Samuel 1:18–27 likewise uses varied structures. Nor does a Book-of-Song/Jashar
identification independently establish which samples define its style. This
countermodel usefully challenges a unique solar-identity reconstruction; it
does not establish its own religious history or approve missing letters.
