# First Samuel 1 23 and whose word is established

## Decision and translation consequence

POB currently reads **“only may Yahweh establish his word.”** A materially
different Hebrew reading, referring to Hannah's utterance, is published in
4Q51 and agrees with the Greek. This is not merely a hypothetical Hebrew
back-translation. Its priority over the Masoretic reading remains unresolved;
retain the current source and main English provisionally.

The existing note discusses the meaning of “his word” but does not disclose
the attested alternative. A source-variant note is warranted in a subsequent
bounded application; it should not disguise different Hebrew wording as
another translation of the same words. No canonical file changes in this pass.

## Competing explanations

[Aejmelaeus 2019](https://www.academia.edu/62201820/Was_Samuel_Meant_to_Be_a_Nazirite_The_First_Chapter_of_Samuel_and_the_Paradigm_Shift_in_Textual_Study_of_the_Hebrew_Bible),
pp.3–6, favors the Qumran/Greek wording and interprets MT as purposeful
reworking. She argues that the vow connection belongs to the story, not
necessarily to later legal harmonization; note 10 allows Syriac to simplify
the longer expression. The chapter-wide historical argument has separate
[mixed-witness constraints](SAMUEL_1_13_PRAYER_PLUS_2026-10-16.md#chapter-argument-and-the-mixed-qumran-witness).

[Hornkohl 2014](https://www.academia.edu/92423957/Her_Word_versus_His_Establishing_the_Underlying_Text_in_1_Samuel_1_23),
*JBL* 133:465–477, argues the reverse. He relates “his word” to Eli's blessing
and the child's survival until dedication (pp.474–475), questions deriving
Syriac from the longer phrase (pp.470–473), and proposes legal clarification
behind the Qumran/Greek reading (pp.476–477). His conclusion does not depend
entirely on that legal reconstruction. Root and one bounded independent reader
read the complete indexed article and all 46 notes; neither claims a PDF
consultation or independent verification of its cited editions.

## Local controls on the proposed history

The retained Hebrew of 1:11 distinguishes the requested child from Hannah's
promised dedication. Eli's 1:17 speech mentions her petition; 1:27 associates
the granted petition with this child. Survival through dedication is a
possible broader implication, not a separately stated promise. Making MT
intelligible therefore removes an argument for necessary emendation without
establishing its originality.

More concretely, Numbers 30:8–9, 12–15 in POB/WLC numbering makes the husband's
hearing the relevant occasion and permits silence to confirm a vow. It does
not explicitly require his presence at its first utterance. Hannah already
informs Elkanah of her permanent dedication plan in MT 1:22, and he permits
her proposed course in 1:23 before the disputed clause. That permission need
not formally ratify every vow condition. Consequently the extra wording
is not demonstrably necessary to supply legal ratification. This weakens one
proposed motivation for secondary expansion, not every possible explanation
of expansion. The disputed clause also keeps Yahweh, not Elkanah, as subject
of the establishing verb; legal resonance and a formal ratification formula
must not simply be identified.

Neither narrative attractiveness, a difficult reading, nor the manuscript's
general reputation decides transmission direction. Both reconstructions must
explain all three distinct expressions. A hypothetical Hebrew דברך behind
Syriac is not a third surviving Hebrew witness.
