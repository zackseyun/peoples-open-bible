# Psalm 14 ancient quotations and transmission

## Judgment

Justin and Jerome strengthen the case for Pauline influence on the longer
Greek Psalm 13, but do not recover an earlier Hebrew text. Justin's surviving
wording is related to the Romans chain, not its verbatim reproduction. Jerome
explicitly proposes incorporation from Paul into the psalm. Retain POB's
distinct Hebrew Psalm 14 and Greek Romans 3; no source, English, note, layout
or canon change follows.

## Justin quotes a related but shorter chain

The institutional [HODOI Greek transcription of Dialogue 27](https://hodoi.fltr.ucl.ac.be/concordances/justin_martyr_tryphon/lecture/27.htm)
places the chain in a dispute about selective prophetic quotation and Sabbath
commands. At 27.3, the local introduction says that he cries out; it does not
explicitly name Paul, Romans or Psalm 13. That absence does not establish
independence from Paul.

| Feature | Justin 27.3 in HODOI | Selected POB Romans chain |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opening predicate | One who understands, συνίων | Romans 3:12 has one who does good; understands occurs at 3:11 |
| Tongues and throat | Tongues precede throat | Throat precedes tongues at 3:13 |
| Cursing, swift feet and fear clauses | Not in this local chain | Present at 3:14, 3:15 and 3:18 |
| Final verb about peace | ἔγνωσαν | Same verb at 3:17 |

The combination is more informative than an isolated shared phrase. It is
compatible with Pauline-related quotation adapted from memory; the omissions
and reversal do not disprove dependence. Conversely, describing Justin as an
exact second-century copy of the complete Romans expansion would erase real
differences and confuse the date of a work with its surviving transmission.

HODOI is a modern transcription, not an ancient manuscript image. Its linked
source is the Philippe Remacle site; the underlying printed Greek edition and
full critical apparatus have not been established here. Preserve its ἄρα
against the ἅμα printed in Meiser's comparison rather than silently combining
the editions into a single diplomatic text.

## Meiser argues for dependence despite the differences

Martin Meiser's [2013 essay](https://publikationen.uni-tuebingen.de/xmlui/handle/10900/162386),
*Die Septuaginta-Zitate des Neuen Testaments bei Justin*, pp. 323–348, makes
a strong dependence argument at pp. 339–341. He notes the repeated πάντες,
the understanding predicate, a different verb form and the reversed clauses;
he proposes memory quotation. He also emphasizes the peace verb shared with
Romans and the expanded psalm rather than the Isaiah form. His conclusion is
that Justin knew Pauline letters; the absence of Paul's name can be explained
by the apologetic setting, not taken as decisive contrary evidence.

His opening discussion, pp. 323–326, distinguishes the work from Parisinus
gr. 450, dated 1363, and a 1541 daughter copy. He considers possible scribal
changes without treating the whole text as wholesale biblical harmonization.
He also discusses modern editorial corrections, including at 27.3. These are
reported transmission controls, not fresh inspection of those manuscripts or
of Marcovich's and Bobichon's apparatuses.

The complete relevant pages 323–326 and 339–341 were read; pp. 339–341 were
also visually inspected. The other pages of the 26-page essay are not claimed
as read. This supports a probable Pauline relationship, not a demonstration
of the first copying event or exclusion of every earlier collection.

## Jerome expressly argues that the chain entered the psalm

In the [preface to book 16 of his Isaiah commentary](https://khazarzar.skeptik.net/books/hieronym/comm_isa/isa16.htm),
Jerome describes Eustochium's challenge to his account of apostolic quotations:
Paul uses eight lines read in churches in Psalm 13 but absent from Hebrew.
He traces their components to Psalms 5, 139, 9 and 35 and Isaiah, discussing
Paul's adaptation of singular wording to plural. These are his psalm numbers.

Jerome does more than identify parallels. He argues that people who did not
understand Paul's weaving of passages found a place in Psalm 13 for the
quotation, rather than Paul drawing these non-Hebrew lines from that psalm.
He reports Greek commentators marking them with an obelus and passing over
them. His distinction between the Septuagint and the common Greek edition
matters: the Vulgata here is expressly called Greek κοινή, not the Latin
Vulgate. Do not count every copy of a longer Greek psalm as Jerome's evidence
for the earliest Septuagint.

The complete relevant Latin preface was read in this presentation and in
[Monumenta](https://monumenta.ch/latein/text.php?domain=&hide_apparatus=&inframe=1&lang=0&level=&links=&rumpfid=Hieronymus%2C+Commentarii%2C+in+Isaiam&tabelle=Hieronymus&translation_links=1).
These are two presentations of one work, not two ancient witnesses. Editorial
bracketed notes are not Jerome's prose. His theological defense and historical
explanation are evidence to assess, not our independent verification of every
Greek commentator or surviving exemplar. No Origen original is newly consulted.

## Consequence and stopping point

Our inference is now more favorable to Pauline-related expansion than a bare
word match alone: Justin supplies a related reception form and Jerome supplies
an explicit ancient directional explanation. Neither supplies pre-Pauline
Hebrew ink, dates the lost exemplar of Papyrus 230, or settles the original
Psalm 14 junction. The opposing earlier-collection hypothesis remains a
historical possibility, not an attested recovered Hebrew reading.

Close this selected quotation check. Reopen only for evidence that can
discriminate the hypotheses, such as the original 11Q7 reconstruction rationale
or a materially earlier independent witness. More paraphrases of these same
authors, another consensus loop or a new inline display would not advance that
question. Quotation and reception alone also do not establish a new canon.
