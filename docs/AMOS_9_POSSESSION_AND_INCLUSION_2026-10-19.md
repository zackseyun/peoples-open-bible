# Amos 9 possession and the inclusion of nations

Date: 2026-10-19. Baseline: `74999d66a6312872868fc549334bfea03b8e03bf`.

## Judgment

Hebrew Amos 9:12 does not by itself specify a military mechanism, but its
wording does not expressly say that the nations voluntarily convert. Retain
POB's open “possess” and the separate humanity/seeking wording in Acts.
Neither “conquer” nor “convert” is a required lexical replacement. The
[earlier textual comparison](AMOS_9_12_SOURCE_COMPARISON_2026-09-06.md)
and its qualified reader disclosure remain intact; historical priority is open.

This is a test of meaning under the retained Hebrew, not another vote on the
same Mur. 88 or Greek readings. No source, main English, reader note, approval
metadata, layout or canon status changes.

## An original inclusion argument

David M. King, [“The Use of Amos 9:11–12 in Acts 15:16–18”](https://biblicalstudies.gospelstudies.org.uk/pdf/ashland_theological_journal/21-1_08.pdf),
*Ashland Theological Journal*21 (1989), 8–13, argues that inclusion in God's
people is already compatible with the Hebrew and need not depend on the Greek
change. Root read all six pages and notes in extraction and complete-page
views. This is an original interpretive essay, not a critical edition or a
survey of scholarly agreement. Its name-clause argument on10 and conclusion
on12 deserve testing independently of its theological framing.

Some of its textual premises need qualification. On11, changing Edom to
humanity is described as a vowels-only change; written WLC has `אדום`, with
vav, and the full proposed difference also involves the verb, object marker
and syntax. That explanation is not established merely by naming similar
letters. Its claim that the Greek is a mistaken reading rather than editorial
interpretation is a transmission hypothesis, not an observed event. Its
citations to Kaiser, Hartley and other works are references within this essay,
not fresh consultations of those originals here.

Daniel Timmer's 2019 study, DOI10.5325/bullbiblrese.29.4.0468, remains unread
in full. The author-posted ResearchGate listing supplied an abstract and public
download link, but retrieval failed and its normal browser page required a
security check. The publisher DOI route was also unavailable. No challenge was
bypassed, and the abstract was not substituted for the full argument.

## Direct Hebrew controls

Root read written WLC Amos9:7–15 and the complete local contexts below. These
are selected counterexamples and parallels, not a full lexical-corpus census
or a new HALOT/TWOT consultation.

| Control | What the actual wording establishes | Limit on transfer to Amos |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Genesis15:3–4 | The house-born person and promised offspring can “inherit me/you,” using ירש with a personal object | The verb is not intrinsically military; this household inheritance does not establish how nations are possessed |
| Isaiah54:1–3 | Descendants possess/inherit nations and inhabit desolate cities | A close restoration parallel, but the clause does not expressly specify conversion or exclude force |
| 2Samuel12:27–29 | Joab links capturing a city with his name being called over it | The general naming construction can occur in conquest; Joab's name is not Yahweh's, so this does not prove an identical divine-name usage |
| Jeremiah7:10–14 | The house over which Yahweh's name is called remains exposed to judgment, with Shiloh as the warning | Divine identification does not itself guarantee favor or safety; this is not a nations-conversion parallel |

Amos9:8–10 distinguishes preservation of Jacob's house from judgment on
sinners, and9:14–15 promises Israel restored settlement. This supports a
restoration setting but does not turn every surviving foreign group into an
explicitly repentant community. In9:12, the nations remain objects of the
possession verb and are identified by Yahweh's name. That combination makes
integration under divine rule a serious interpretation without establishing
its political mechanism or a complete later ecclesial identity.

## Translation consequence

The weaker conclusion survives: a Hebrew-based reading can allow more than
hostile land acquisition. The stronger claim that the name clause alone proves
voluntary religious incorporation does not follow from these controls. Nor
does the personal object of ירש require conquest in every context.

“Possess” preserves the action without inserting either mechanism. POB's
existing interpretation remains provisional, not an exclusively military
lexical definition. Changing it to “convert,” deleting Edom, or importing
“seek the Lord” would exceed this evidence. No exact revised verse is proposed
or approved. A change to historical source selection still requires the
earlier dossier's discriminating Hebrew/Greek evidence; a stronger semantic
claim needs a broader, construction-sensitive lexical argument. Park these
completed article/control readings rather than repeating them as a new test.
