| Selected unit | Alternative analysis | Operations required |
| --- | --- | --- |
| לְאִיתִיאֵל, twice | לָאִיתִי אֵל: a first-person perfect of לאה, followed by a vocative | Change vowels and word division; reassess accents and the prefix analysis. |

| וְאֻכָל | First-person ability verb from יכל | Reanalyse the word; defective spelling and pausal vocalization can accommodate a verbal reading. |

These proposals can preserve the consonantal sequence
**לאיתיאל לאיתיאל ואכל**. That does not make them the same selected Hebrew
interpretation. The weariness proposal changes both word divisions and vowels.
The shortened כלה proposal must not silently become an unshortened
וְאֶכְלֶה with an additional final ה. Nor is an ordinary passive of אכל
interchangeable with the shortened כלה derivation.

[GKC §75](https://bohuslav.me/gesenius/gesenius/75.html), paragraphs k, o–q and t,
describes apocope in consecutive imperfects, resulting vocalization and
first-person shortened/full comparisons. These rules provide a morphological
route for testing the shortened proposal without adding a consonant. They do
not attest that reading in a manuscript of Proverbs or decide its intention
here. The ability reading likewise supplies no explicit negative: an English
inability claim or an added object would need further justification.

## Strongest cases on both sides

Nehemiah 11:7 actually places the same pointed name inside a genealogy. This
establishes that Ithiel can be a personal name, not that Proverbs names the same
individual. The repeated recipient phrase can resume and expand the address
to include Ucal. Neither recipient is explicitly called a son or disciple.
This is a positive syntactic case for names, not merely reliance on the XML tags.

The verbal reading has a genuine contextual advantage: repeated weariness can
lead naturally into verse 2's explanation of limited human understanding and
verse 4's questions about knowledge beyond human capacity. Direct address to
God occurs later in verses 7–9. Nevertheless, verse 2 can also explain the
speaker's discourse after a superscription. Its introductory כִּי does not
force an assertion of weariness in the preceding clause. Repetition is awkward
enough to investigate, not enough by itself to prove textual corruption.

[McKenzie and Shelton's 2020 article](https://www.sebts.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/McKenzie.pdf),
printed page 11 and notes 23–26, recognizes the name/verbal alternatives and
explicitly identifies changes to vowels and spacing. It argues from prophetic
utterance parallels toward an exhausted-speaker interpretation. That is a
literary argument, not attestation of reconstructed vowels. Its embedded
modern English quotation was encountered during consultation; no new English
draft was prepared from it. References to Fox and Krantz are citations in the
article, not works independently consulted here. The article's broader
Davidic/messianic thesis does not determine this local source decision.

The Latin tail has “cum quo est Deus et qui Deo secum morante confortatus ait.”
Its relative clauses describe the speaker: God is with him, and he speaks as
one strengthened while God remains with him. `Deo … morante` is an ablative
absolute; `confortatus` is a passive participle, not an assertion of weariness.
This reading does not explicitly address two named recipients, either. It is
a distinct interpretation rather than a literal match for POB's names or its
repeated weary vocatives. Associations with the name's meaning and an ability
reading are possible explanations, not proved word-for-word Hebrew alignment.
Latin strengthening does not establish Hebrew vowels, missing consonants or
the exact final-verb derivation. [Weber–Gryson2007 selected text](https://www.die-bibel.de/en/bible/VUL/PRO.30),
30:1; surrounding first-person speech begins in30:2.

The publisher Greek has “τοῖς πιστεύουσιν θεῷ, καὶ παύομαι.” Article plus dative
plural participle addresses those trusting God; the last verb is first-person
present middle, describing ceasing/stopping. This independently checks the
earlier edition-derived digital observation against an identified publisher
edition. It does not create a second independent ancient witness: the editions
are related controls. Greek trust and ceasing do not equal repeated weariness
vocatives, and Latin presence and strengthening do not equal Greek trust and
ceasing. Their exact Hebrew sources remain unproved. [Rahlfs–Hanhart2006 selected text](https://www.die-bibel.de/en/bible/LXX/PRO.30),
30:1.
